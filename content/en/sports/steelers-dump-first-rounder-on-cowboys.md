# Steelers Dump First-Rounder On Cowboys

The **Pittsburgh Steelers** traded **2023 first-round** offensive tackle **Broderick Jones** and a **2027 fourth-round pick** to the **Dallas Cowboys** for **2027 third- and sixth-round picks**, first reported Saturday by **Ian Rapoport**, **Mike Garafolo**, and ESPN's **Jeremy Fowler**. It is the sort of deal that looks small on a transaction wire and large in two buildings: a former top selection moving across conferences on the last full day before the league's roster is required to shrink.

Sunday's **6 p.m. ET** cut deadline is the hour that turns a summer roster into a season. Trades that land on the Saturday before that hour are not accidents. They are the last chance to convert a player who no longer fits into draft capital that still might. Pittsburgh used that hour. Dallas used it too.

## A first-rounder who started, and never stuck

Jones started **38 games in three seasons** but never stuck. That sentence is the entire Pittsburgh evaluation, compressed. Thirty-eight starts is not a washout's résumé. It is the résumé of a player who was good enough to be on the field and never good enough, in the club's judgment, to be the answer.

First-round offensive tackles are drafted to end arguments. They are supposed to lock a side of the line for a decade, eat the best edge rushers on the schedule, and make the quarterback's drop look ordinary. Jones was selected to do that work in 2023. Three seasons later, Pittsburgh is sending him to the NFC East and adding a fourth-round pick to the package to make the math work for Dallas.

The *never stuck* clause is the one that matters in a league that will forgive almost any other first-round sin. Injuries can be waited out. Technique can be coached. A player who starts 38 games and still does not become the starter of record is a different problem. He has been seen. The tape is long. The organization has decided that more time will not produce a different player in black and gold.

That is a colder verdict than a simple injury story, and it is colder than a simple talent story. It is a fit story. Pittsburgh had already spent the spring rearranging the position. Saturday was the paperwork.

## The neck, the fusion, the lost year

A **2025 neck injury** required **spinal fusion** and ended that year early. Spinal fusion is not a bruised-nerve anecdote and not a two-week rest. It is a procedure that joins vertebrae so they no longer move against each other, a last-resort stability play for a position that asks a man to put his head into other large men for a living. For a tackle, the neck is not a peripheral joint. It is the column that has to take the first collision of every snap.

The injury ending 2025 early meant Jones lost the ordinary development year that a former first-rounder is supposed to spend turning starts into mastery. He spent it in an operating room and a rehab protocol instead. That is not a moral failing. It is a medical fact that changes how a front office prices the rest of a contract, and how 31 other front offices price a trade.

Pittsburgh **declined his fifth-year option**. For a first-round pick, that option is the club's cheapest way to keep a player into a fifth season at a predetermined price. Declining it is a public statement. It says the team does not want to be married to him at that number for that year. It does not always mean a trade is coming the next August. In this case, it was the first brick in the path that ended on Saturday.

A declined option also tells the league the clock is visible. Agents notice. Rival scouts notice. The player notices. Once a club has said it will not pick up the cheap fifth year, the remaining question is not whether the player is in the long-term plan. It is whether he is in the short-term plan, and for how many guaranteed roster spots, and at what opportunity cost against a 53-man limit that does not care who was drafted where.

## Fautanu to the left, Iheanachor in the first round

The rest of the path was personnel. Pittsburgh **moved Troy Fautanu to left tackle** and **drafted Max Iheanachor in the first round this spring**. Those are not complementary notes. They are a replacement plan.

Left tackle is the premium side for most offenses, the edge that protects a right-handed quarterback's blind side and commands the highest pay. Moving Fautanu there is a declaration that the job Jones was drafted to grow into is no longer his. Drafting Iheanachor in the first round this spring is the second declaration: the club was willing to spend another high selection on the same position group rather than bet another year on Jones getting all the way back from a fused spine.

A team that has already paid a first-round price once does not spend another first-round pick on the same problem if it still believes the first solution is about to bloom. Iheanachor is the bloom Pittsburgh chose. Fautanu is the veteran of the room who has been asked to take the most important chair. Jones, after 38 starts and a fusion, became the man without a chair.

That is how first-rounders leave. Rarely in a sudden burst of dislike. Usually after a sequence of quieter choices — an option declined, a teammate slid over, a new name called in the first round — that make the old name surplus. Surplus is tradable. Surplus on the Saturday before cuts is urgently tradable.

## 140 snaps as a for-sale sign

Jones played **140 preseason snaps** after being cleared, a tell that Pittsburgh was shopping him ahead of Sunday's 6 p.m. ET cut deadline. Preseason snap counts are a language. Stars sit. Starters get a series or two, enough to knock the rust off and get out. Players on the bubble play. Players being *shown to the league* play a lot.

One hundred forty snaps after medical clearance is not a workload a club gives a man it intends to hide. It is tape, freshly minted, for the other buildings that had questions about a neck and a fusion and a lost 2025. Pittsburgh needed those buildings to see Jones move in live color, against live rushers, after the procedure. Dallas, on Saturday's evidence, saw enough.

The cut deadline makes that tape perishable. A player who is on the 53-man roster after 6 p.m. ET on Sunday is a player the team has chosen to keep. A player who is not must clear waivers or be stashed, and a former first-round tackle with 38 starts is not a candidate to sneak through unclaimed. If Pittsburgh wanted draft capital instead of a waiver claim by a rival, Saturday was the window.

Rapoport, Garafolo, and Fowler — the three names that tend to land on the same NFL transaction when it is real — reported the trade on Saturday, which is how these deals now enter the world: not with a joint press release first, but with a cluster of trusted reporters hitting send at once. The details that followed were the details that matter. The picks were 2027 picks. The extra chip Pittsburgh sent was a fourth. The return was a third and a sixth.

## What Dallas is buying

Dallas needs tackle *insurance* behind **Tyler Guyton** and **Terence Steele** after shaky camps from **Drew Shelton** and **Nate Thomas**. That is a different need than Pittsburgh's. The Cowboys are not, on the available facts, replacing a starter. They are buying a body who has started 38 games and can, if the two names in front of him go down or go backwards, play.

Guyton and Steele are the incumbents. Insurance, in offensive-line language, means the next man has to be able to take an NFL pass set on short notice, not merely survive a practice scout-team look. Shelton and Thomas having shaky camps is the reason that insurance felt urgent rather than theoretical. Camps are where depth charts stop being hypothetical. If the next tackles in a room do not look like they can play, a front office either lives with the risk into September or it goes shopping in August.

**Albert Breer** said Jones will compete at *right tackle* pending a physical. The physical is not a formality after a 2025 spinal fusion. It is the actual trade contingency. Dallas can like the tape from 140 preseason snaps and still need its own doctors to sign off on a neck that has already been surgically stabilized. Until that exam is done, the competition at right tackle is a plan, not a finished depth chart.

Right tackle, rather than left, is the quieter side in most offenses and often the easier place to plug a player who has started a lot without ever becoming immovable. It is also, for a team that already has two names it prefers, a place where a new arrival can compete without detonating the rest of the line. Pittsburgh's problem was left tackle as a long-term identity. Dallas's problem, as stated, is backup quality behind Guyton and Steele. Those are not the same job posting. They can still be solved by the same human being if the physical holds.

## A receiver cut, a prior raid, and a pair of grades

The Cowboys **cut receiver Denzel Mims** to make room. Roster spots are arithmetic. A tackle coming in requires a player going out if the club is already at the relevant limit, and a receiver who had not forced the issue is the sort of name that gets moved to make a trade legal. Mims is not the story of the deal. He is the roster-mechanical cost of it: one fewer wideout so that a former first-round tackle can take a physical and then, if he passes, take a swing at right tackle.

It is the **second straight year** Dallas raided Pittsburgh, after the **2025 George Pickens** deal. That is the part that will travel further than the pick numbers. One trade is a fit. Two consecutive years of Dallas taking a piece off Pittsburgh's roster is a pattern the two fan bases will not need help noticing. Pickens was the skill-position version. Jones is the lineman version. Same direction of travel: north to Texas.

The return for Pittsburgh is a 2027 third-round pick and a 2027 sixth, against Jones plus a 2027 fourth. In draft-value terms, the Steelers are trading a former first-rounder they had already replaced and a mid-round pick for a higher mid-round pick and a late one. They are not getting a 2026 selection. They are getting 2027 capital, which is the currency of a club that has already spent this spring's first-rounder on Iheanachor and already parked Fautanu at left tackle.

**Sports Illustrated** graded Dallas **C+** and Pittsburgh **B+**. Those letters are not a medical report and not a scouting report. They are a snapshot of how the industry reads the risk on the day the trade lands. A C+ for Dallas is the grade you give a team that added a former first-round starter with a fused spine, a declined fifth-year option, and a physical still to pass — useful if the neck holds and the camps of Shelton and Thomas were as shaky as they looked, expensive in opportunity cost if the physical or the tape says otherwise. A B+ for Pittsburgh is the grade you give a team that extracted a third-round pick for a player it had already written out of its future, on the eve of a cut deadline, after 140 snaps of advertising.

The grades will look smart or foolish the first time Jones has to take a full-speed NFL bull rush on an island in Dallas, and the first time Iheanachor or Fautanu has to do the same in Pittsburgh. Saturday's facts do not include those snaps. They include a trade first reported by Rapoport, Garafolo, and Fowler; a neck that required fusion; a deadline at 6 p.m. ET on Sunday; a receiver named Mims coming off the Cowboys' roster; a prior Pickens raid; and a pair of letters, C+ and B+, hanging on the deal like price tags. The rest is the season.
