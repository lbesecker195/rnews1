# Hrgovic Stuns Itauma For IBF Belt

**Filip Hrgovic** stopped **21-year-old Moses Itauma** at **2:27 of round nine** Saturday at London's *sold-out* **O2 Arena**, taking the **vacant IBF heavyweight title** and becoming **Croatia's first world heavyweight champion**. The result inverted every assumption that had followed the Brit into the ring. Itauma entered **14-0 with 12 knockouts**, a **9-1 favorite** chasing the **second-youngest heavyweight belt after Mike Tyson**. He dropped **34-year-old** Hrgovic (**21-1, 16 KOs**) in round two and won the early rounds. He had never been past six. By the ninth he was out on his feet. Trainer **Ben Davison** threw in the towel as referee **Howard Foster** waved it off. Itauma left on a stretcher. Promoter **Frank Warren** cited a late-round leg injury. Hrgovic, who said he had been "outboxed," told the ring: *"I am the present. He is definitely the future."*

That sentence is the whole night, compressed. The favorite was supposed to be the future arriving early. The veteran was supposed to be the obstacle. The obstacle became the champion. The future left on a stretcher.

## A sold-out O2 and a vacant IBF belt

London's O2 Arena was sold out Saturday for a vacant IBF heavyweight title. Vacant belts draw a particular kind of crowd: people who want to see a lineage start, not merely continue. There was no champion to dethrone in the usual sense. There was a strap with no owner and two men who wanted to be the first to wear it in this cycle. One of them was 21. One of them was 34. The age gap is the plot.

Itauma is British. Home arenas amplify favorites. A 9-1 favorite in London, in a building that had no empty seats, chasing a piece of history that only **Mike Tyson** had reached younger, is as close as boxing gets to a coronation script. The second-youngest heavyweight belt after Tyson is not a casual talking point. It is the kind of marker that follows a fighter for the rest of a career if he gets it, and for a long time if he does not. Saturday was supposed to be the night the marker stuck to Itauma.

Hrgovic arrived as the other name on the vacant-title poster: older, already 21-1 with 16 knockouts, a Croatian heavyweight who had not yet become a world champion. Croatia had never had a world heavyweight champion. That fact sat in the background of the billing, easy to treat as color until it became the lead.

The vacant IBF heavyweight title is not a regional bauble. It is a world championship in the division that still organizes the sport's top of the mountain. Stopping a 21-year-old unbeaten favorite for that belt, in London, in a sold-out O2, is how a 34-year-old writes himself into a national first. Hrgovic did not win a decision in a quiet hall. He stopped Itauma at 2:27 of round nine, with the building watching the favorite go from early control to a towel in the ring.

## The 9-1 favorite who had never been past six

Itauma entered 14-0 with 12 knockouts. Those numbers describe a finisher. Twelve stoppages in 14 wins is a rate that trains crowds to expect an ending before a distance. It also trains a young heavyweight's body for a certain kind of night: explosive, early, conclusive. The other number that mattered Saturday was the one that was not on the record. He had never been past six.

Never been past six, against a 34-year-old who had been 21-1 with 16 knockouts of his own, in a scheduled fight for a vacant world title, is a structural problem hiding inside a highlight reel. Early-round knockouts do not teach a fighter how the eighth feels. They do not teach him how the ninth feels when the tank is empty and the other man is still there. They teach him that nights end when he decides they end. Saturday did not end when he decided.

The 9-1 line said the market agreed with the record. Favorites at that price are not supposed to need a late-round education. They are supposed to be the ones administering it. Itauma was chasing history that only Tyson had reached at a younger age. The Brit was the future in the present tense, at home, unbeaten, a knockout artist, a 9-1 choice. Hrgovic was the veteran assignment: 34, already carrying a loss on the card, there to be the name a young champion beats on the way to the rest of the division.

The early rounds followed the script the odds had purchased.

## Round two: the Brit drops the 34-year-old

The Brit dropped 34-year-old Hrgovic in round two. A knockdown in the second round of a vacant-title fight, delivered by the 21-year-old favorite to the 34-year-old, is the moment a sold-out O2 decides it is watching the coronation it paid for. Hrgovic went down. Itauma, 14-0 with 12 knockouts, had done the thing his record said he would do: put the other man on the canvas early.

He won the early rounds. That is not a polite hedge. The Brit won them. He had Hrgovic down in the second and he had the rounds that followed in the shape a favorite is supposed to have them. For a fighter who had never been past six, the early rounds are the whole known world. He was living in that world, and he was winning it.

Hrgovic's record coming in was 21-1 with 16 knockouts. A man with 16 knockouts is not a durable statue by trade. He is a puncher who has ended nights. Being dropped in round two, in London, against a 9-1 favorite, is the sort of hole that swallows veterans who have already been 21-1 and are not supposed to become Croatia's first anything. The knockdown was real. The early rounds were Itauma's. The night was still young in the only way that would eventually matter: it was not yet past six.

What the early rounds did not do was end the fight. Hrgovic got up. The vacant IBF heavyweight title was still vacant. The O2 was still sold out. The 21-year-old had scored the knockdown and won the early rounds and still had to keep doing it into a distance he had never seen.

## A fifth-round uppercut, flush, and a walk-through

A fifth-round uppercut landed flush. In heavyweight boxing, a flush uppercut in the fifth is often the punch that converts an early knockdown into a stoppage. It is the shot that finds a man who has already been down and finishes the argument. Itauma threw it. It landed flush.

Hrgovic walked through it.

That is the turn the rest of the night hangs on. Not the knockdown in round two, which the favorite was supposed to score. Not the early rounds, which the 9-1 man was supposed to win. The fifth-round uppercut that landed flush, and the 34-year-old walking through it, is the moment the distance Itauma had never seen began to become the story. A fighter who has never been past six has a fifth round. He does not necessarily have an answer for a man who absorbs the flush uppercut and remains in front of him.

Walking through a flush uppercut is not the same as not getting hit. Hrgovic got hit. The punch landed flush. He walked through it anyway. For a 21-year-old who finishes people — 12 knockouts in 14 wins — a punch that lands flush and does not end the night is a new kind of information. It arrives in the fifth, one round short of the farthest he had ever gone, with the later rounds still ahead, and with a Croatian who had been down in the second still coming forward.

The vacant belt does not care who won the early rounds if the later rounds belong to someone else. The fifth-round uppercut was supposed to be the bridge from early control to a stoppage. Hrgovic made it a bridge to the second half of a fight Itauma had never fought.

## The eighth: the tank empties

Itauma emptied his tank in the eighth.

He had never been past six. The eighth is two rounds beyond that frontier. Emptying a tank in the eighth, after winning the early rounds, after a knockdown in the second, after a flush uppercut in the fifth that did not finish the job, is what a young knockout artist's body does when the night refuses to end on the usual schedule. The fuel that produces 12 stoppages in 14 fights is finite. It is spent in the work of dropping a 34-year-old, of winning those early rounds, of throwing the uppercut that lands flush. When the other man walks through it, the tank does not refill. It empties.

Round eight is where a vacant IBF heavyweight title fight stops being a prospect's highlight and becomes a veteran’s distance. Hrgovic was 34. He had been 21-1 with 16 knockouts. He had been dropped and outboxed, by his own later admission. He was still there in the eighth, in a sold-out O2, with the British favorite's tank going empty in front of a home crowd that had come to see the second-youngest heavyweight champion after Tyson.

Emptying the tank is not a tactical choice. It is what happens when a fighter who has never been past six is still throwing in the eighth as if the sixth were the horizon. The favorite had done the hard early work. He had not saved anything for the rounds he had never visited. Hrgovic, who had walked through the fifth-round uppercut, was in position to receive a ninth that the 21-year-old could no longer own.

## Round nine, 2:27: the towel, the referee, the stretcher

In the ninth he was out on his feet. That is the sentence that follows an emptied tank as surely as a knockdown follows a clean shot. Itauma, 21, 14-0 with 12 knockouts when the night began, was out on his feet in round nine in London. The 9-1 favorite was no longer winning early rounds. He was no longer the man who had dropped Hrgovic in the second. He was a fighter past six, past eight, and past the point of answering.

Trainer Ben Davison threw in the towel as referee Howard Foster waved it off. The stoppage was official at 2:27 of round nine. Two people ended the fight in the same motion: the trainer who had seen enough of his fighter out on his feet, and the referee who waved it off as the towel came. That is how a vacant IBF heavyweight title changes hands without a scorecard. Hrgovic stopped Itauma. Croatia had a world heavyweight champion for the first time.

Itauma left on a stretcher. The image is the opposite of the coronation the 9-1 line had implied. A young British heavyweight chasing Tyson’s company on the age-and-belt list does not leave a sold-out O2 on a stretcher in the story the crowd bought. He leaves with a belt. Saturday, the stretcher was the last transportation the favorite used in the arena.

Promoter Frank Warren cited a late-round leg injury. That is the promoter's account of what contributed to the late-round collapse: a leg injury, late. It sits beside the other late-round facts without replacing them. Itauma had never been past six. He emptied his tank in the eighth. In the ninth he was out on his feet. Davison threw in the towel. Foster waved it off at 2:27. The stretcher followed. Warren's citation of a late-round leg injury is part of the night's record, not a substitute for the stoppage.

The vacant title was vacant no longer. Hrgovic, 34, 21-1 with 16 knockouts coming in, had taken it by stoppage from a 21-year-old who had dropped him, outboxed him, and then run out of the fight he had never had to finish.

## "I am the present. He is definitely the future."

Hrgovic said he had been "outboxed." The quote is not a victory lap. It is an admission from the new champion that the early rounds, and the boxing in them, belonged to Itauma. The Brit dropped him in round two. The Brit won the early rounds. A fifth-round uppercut landed flush. A man who says he was outboxed is describing that stretch, not inventing a different fight. The 34-year-old did not claim he had been the better boxer from the opening bell. He claimed the ending.

He told the ring: "I am the present. He is definitely the future."

The line sorts the night into two tenses and gives each man one of them. Hrgovic is the present: Croatia's first world heavyweight champion, holder of the IBF belt that was vacant when Saturday started, winner by stoppage at 2:27 of round nine in a sold-out O2 Arena in London. Itauma is definitely the future: 21 years old, still the fighter who entered 14-0 with 12 knockouts, still the man who had been a 9-1 favorite chasing the second-youngest heavyweight belt after Mike Tyson, still the Brit who could drop a 34-year-old in round two and win the rounds that followed. The present is a title. The future, on this night, left on a stretcher with a trainer's towel as the last argument.

That framing is generous from a man who had just been the obstacle. It is also accurate to the facts that survived the stoppage. Nothing about Itauma's age changed at 2:27 of the ninth. Nothing about Hrgovic's age changed either. The vacant belt changed owners. The 9-1 favorite did not become a former prospect; he became a 21-year-old who had never been past six and then was, and then was stopped. Davison's towel and Foster's wave made the present official. Warren's note of a late-round leg injury attached a medical clause to the future's exit.

Croatia had never had a world heavyweight champion. Hrgovic is the first. He is 34. He was dropped in round two. He was outboxed, by his own word. He walked through a fifth-round uppercut that landed flush. He was still there when the tank emptied in the eighth and when the ninth went to 2:27. The IBF heavyweight title is his. The sold-out O2 saw a favorite's coronation turn into a veteran's national first.

The present, as Hrgovic defined it from the ring, is the man who stopped the fight. The future, as he defined it in the same breath, is the 21-year-old who made him say he had been outboxed. Boxing rarely hands both sentences to the winner on the same night. Saturday at the O2, after a knockdown, a flush uppercut, an emptied tank, a towel, a referee's wave, and a stretcher, both sentences were true at once.
