# Angels Blow Ninth After Robbing Homer

The **Los Angeles Angels** took a **3-1** lead into the ninth Friday at **Angel Stadium** with a **96.5%** win probability after **Luis Arraez**'s leadoff groundout — then tied a record that dates to at least **1900**. The sequence that followed was not a slow leak. It was a closer hitting a man, walking another, surviving a ball that **Statcast** said would have been a homer in **26 of 30** parks, then handing the game to a pinch-hitter's first career grand slam.

**Philadelphia** won **5-3**. The **AL-worst** Angels fell to **52-83**. The **75-60** Phillies tightened their wild-card hold. Those are the box-score facts. The story is how a 96.5 percent game became a museum piece.

## A 96.5 percent lead that would not stay closed

Win probability is a cold instrument. It does not care that a team is already last in the American League. It cares about inning, score, outs, and base state. After Arraez, the Phillies' leadoff man in the ninth, grounded out, the model sitting on Friday night's broadcast graphic said the Angels had a 96.5 percent chance to win. One out. Two-run lead. Home closer on the mound in Anaheim. That is supposed to be a handshake.

Closer **Ben Joyce** then hit **Alec Bohm** and walked **Justin Crawford**. The lead was still 3-1. The out was still on the board. The win probability was no longer a formality. A hit batter and a walk are how ninth innings stop being simple. They put the tying run on the bases without requiring a ball to be hit well. They also put a closer into the exact mental weather he is paid to avoid: extra pitches, extra traffic, a lineup that can suddenly see him.

Joyce's job, at that point, was still the job he entered to do. Get three outs before two runs. He had one of the outs. He had created two of the baserunners. The next pitch would decide whether the inning stayed a scare or became a disaster.

## A homer in 26 parks, and a robbery in this one

**Bryson Stott** crushed a full-count fastball that Statcast said would have been a homer in 26 of 30 parks. That is not a warning-track fly. That is a ball with homer distance in the vast majority of the sport's buildings, a ball that, in a different outfield geometry, is 4-3 Philadelphia and a closer walking off to a different set of boos.

**Wade Meckler** robbed it at the short right-field wall. Angel Stadium's right field is not a canyon. The wall is short enough that a leaping outfielder can still get a glove above the line that Statcast treats as gone in 26 other places. Meckler got there. The ball that should have tied the game, or worse, became an out. Two down. Still 3-1. The robbery was the kind of play a losing season is supposed to be allowed to keep.

For a moment, the inning looked like a story about a save that got interesting. Hit batter. Walk. Full-count missile. A robbery. Closers live through that and are called resilient. Teams at 52-83 almost never get to be called resilient. They needed this one to end there.

It did not end there.

## Wild pitch, walk, bases loaded

Joyce then wild-pitched and walked **J.T. Realmuto** to load the bases. The wild pitch moved the runners. The walk to Realmuto filled the last empty bag. Two outs, bases loaded, still 3-1, and the Angels' closer had now hit a man, walked two, thrown a wild pitch, and survived only because Meckler had taken a homer off the board at the short wall.

That is the inning in which a manager stops hoping the original pitcher will finish what he started. Traffic like that is not a one-pitch problem. It is a sequence problem. Every additional hitter sees a closer who has already missed the zone, already plunked someone, already bounced a ball that moved runners. The Phillies, at 75-60 and hunting a wild-card hold, did not need to be reminded to keep the line moving.

The robbery had bought the Angels an out. It had not bought them a clean inning. Win probability, which had sat at 96.5 percent after the Arraez groundout, was still an Angels number — later, at the moment before the swing that ended it, **85.6%** — but 85.6 is not 96.5. The gap between those two figures is the hit batter, the walks, the wild pitch, and the fact that the bases were now full with two outs.

## Suzuki calls lefty; Mattingly answers righty

Manager **Kurt Suzuki** called for lefty **Sammy Peralta** to face **Brandon Marsh**. That is the book. Marsh, a left-handed bat, against a left-handed pitcher, with the bases loaded and the season already heavy enough. Suzuki, in the ninth of a 3-1 game, was playing the platoon. He was also conceding that Joyce's night was over. The closer had done the damage. The lefty was supposed to be the solvent.

**Don Mattingly** pinch-hit righty **Edmundo Sosa**. That is the answering book. If the home team will throw a lefty, the visiting team will take the left-handed hitter out of the box and put a right-handed one in it. Sosa was that right-handed one. Mattingly, managing a club that entered the night at 75-60, used a bench piece the way a wild-card race requires: not to fill an at-bat, but to change the geometry of the at-bat.

The matchup Suzuki wanted was Peralta versus Marsh. The matchup he got was Peralta versus Sosa. Baseball games in September — and late August, when the standings already look like September — are decided in that gap between the pitcher a manager summons and the hitter the other manager produces.

Sosa launched a **404-foot grand slam** — *his first* — flipping win probability from **85.6% Angels** to **91.4% Phillies**. Four hundred four feet is not a cheap porch job. It is a ball that does not need a short wall and does not need Statcast to hold a committee meeting about 26 of 30 parks. It is gone in the park where it was hit. It scored Bohm, Crawford, Realmuto, and Sosa. The 3-1 lead was a 5-3 deficit before the ball came down.

A first career grand slam is a sentence a player gets to keep. Sosa got his on a pinch-hit, against a lefty summoned specifically to face someone else, in a ninth inning that had already featured a robbery. The Phillies did not need it to be poetic. They needed it to travel 404 feet. It did.

## Eleven blown ninths, a record from 1900

**CBS Sports Research** says the **2026 Angels** now have **11** losses after taking a ninth-inning lead, tying the **2012 Brewers** and **1978 Mets**. The record dates to at least 1900. That is not a local embarrassment. It is a historical category. For as long as the sport has been keeping a version of this book, three teams share the mark for turning a ninth-inning lead into a loss this many times in a season: Milwaukee in 2012, New York in 1978, and these Angels.

Eleven is not a bad week. Eleven is a season-long habit. It means the 3-1 lead on Friday was not an isolated collapse. It was the latest instance of a team that has spent 2026 taking leads into the ninth and failing to keep them. Closer nights, bullpen nights, walk nights, wild-pitch nights — the methods can change. The result, 11 times, has not.

Tying the 2012 Brewers and 1978 Mets is the sort of company a franchise does not put on a promotional schedule. Those clubs are remembered, in this specific ledger, for not being able to finish. The 2026 Angels are now on that list with a month of regular season still to slog through. Records that date to 1900 are not supposed to be available to a modern bullpen with Statcast and a six-man relief map. This one was available. They tied it.

The 96.5 percent figure is how you know Friday was not a tightrope they walked all night. They were almost done. Arraez had grounded out. Meckler had robbed Stott. The universe had already given them the out on the ball that would have been a homer in 26 parks. They still found a way to hand **Don Mattingly** a pinch-hit grand slam.

## 52-83 against a wild-card clock

The AL-worst Angels fell to 52-83. Last place is not a night. It is an average. A team that loses like this in the ninth, on top of everything else that produces a 52-83 record, is a team whose season has been decided in the other direction for a long time. Friday did not eliminate them from a race they were in. It added another loss to a pile that already described the worst club in the league, and it did it in the loudest possible inning.

The 75-60 Phillies tightened their wild-card hold. That is the other half of the same box score. Philadelphia did not need a pretty ninth. It needed four runs and a road win against a last-place team that had done almost everything except close. A wild-card hold is a standings phrase: not a division title, not a bye, but the difference between October and golf. At 75-60, every game that a club in their neighborhood loses is a game they would like to have already banked. They banked this one on Sosa's first grand slam.

Angel Stadium has seen better ninths and better years. It has a short right-field wall that, on Friday, produced the play of the night and then watched that play become a footnote. Meckler's robbery will live in a highlight. Joyce's hit batter, his walks, his wild pitch, and Peralta's 404-foot mistake will live in the loss column. Suzuki played the lefty. Mattingly played the righty. The righty won.

There is no need to invent a larger moral. The facts are already enough, and they are already historic. A 3-1 lead. A 96.5 percent win probability after a leadoff groundout. A closer who hit Bohm and walked Crawford. A Stott fastball that Statcast said was a homer in 26 of 30 parks, pulled back by Meckler. A wild pitch. A walk to Realmuto. A lefty named Peralta. A pinch-hitter named Sosa. Four hundred four feet. 5-3. Eleven times. 1900. 2012. 1978. 52-83. 75-60. The Angels robbed a homer and still blew the ninth. The Phillies, as they have to in a wild-card race, took it.
