# Cruise Recruits Hathaway For Thunder Sequel

Hollywood has spent the better part of four decades treating *Days of Thunder* as a period piece that happened to mint a star marriage and a NASCAR aesthetic. On Saturday, **Paramount** treated it as a living franchise. The studio locked a sequel that pairs **Tom Cruise** with **Anne Hathaway**, plants a **June 2, 2028** theatrical date on the calendar, and hands the keys back to Cole Trickle — the cocky stock-car driver Cruise first played in **Tony Scott**'s 1990 racing film. Plot details stay sealed. The image that announced the movie does not.

Cruise posted it himself: the two A-listers leaning on a NASCAR stock car in Daytona, the most famous stretch of American asphalt that a studio camera can rent. The photograph does the work a teaser trailer usually does. It says the sequel is real, that Hathaway is in it, and that the production intends to look like a race weekend rather than a soundstage approximation of one. Nearly *38 years* after Trickle first hit the track, the first Cruise-Hathaway pairing is officially a studio priority.

## A Sequel That Waited Almost Four Decades

*Days of Thunder* arrived in the summer of 1990 as a cousin to *Top Gun*: a Tony Scott picture about speed, ego, and the expensive machinery required to turn both into entertainment. Cruise's Cole Trickle was a talented outsider dropped into the stock-car circuit, a character built from swagger and a crash-prone learning curve. The film took **$82.6 million domestic** and **$157.9 million worldwide**, respectable numbers for a racing drama that was never going to outrun the decade's comic-book and action tentpoles, and more than enough to keep the title in circulation as a cable perennial and a NASCAR-adjacent pop artifact.

### The Kidman Footnote and the Box Office

It also became, in the gossip pages, the set where Cruise met **Nicole Kidman**. That biographical footnote has outlived most of the original's racing sequences. For a generation of viewers, *Days of Thunder* is less a motorsport movie than the origin story of a very public marriage. The sequel cannot undo that history, and it does not need to. What it can do is treat Trickle as a character who aged in public along with the actor who played him.

Hollywood has tried, at intervals, to revive racing as a big-screen genre. Some of those attempts leaned on documentary grit. Others leaned on digital cars that never quite sat on the pavement. A *Days of Thunder* sequel has a different advantage: a star who still sells physical spectacle, a title that still means something to people who watched it on VHS, and a sport that now commands stadium-sized television audiences. NASCAR in 2026 is not the regional curiosity it was when Scott's cameras first rolled through the infield. It is a national entertainment product with a built-in visual language — the cars, the pits, the oval, the noise — that IMAX cameras were more or less invented to swallow.

## Hathaway as Owner and Engineer

Hathaway plays a **team owner and engineer**. That is the only role description Paramount has allowed out of the garage. It is also a smarter piece of casting logic than it first appears. The original film's dramatic engine was a driver learning to listen. A sequel that puts an A-list actress in the owner's box, and in the engineering notes, shifts the power off the track and into the people who decide whether a car even rolls onto it.

She is coming off a run that would look crowded on anyone else's calendar: ***The Devil Wears Prada 2***, ***The Odyssey***, and ***The End of Oak Street***, with ***Verity*** also due **Oct. 2**. That is four titles in the same orbit as a NASCAR sequel she has not even started shooting in public. Hathaway has spent two decades proving she can move between studio comedy, prestige drama, and the occasional musical without losing the audience. Putting her opposite Cruise is not a stunt pairing so much as a recognition that the sequel needs a second center of gravity. A racing movie that is only about the man in the car is a 1990 movie. A racing movie about the woman who owns the team is a 2028 movie that still gets to use the 1990 title.

It will also be the *first Cruise-Hathaway pairing*. That fact is easy to overlook because both actors have been famous for so long that the public assumes they have already shared a frame. They have not. Cruise's recent collaborators have tended to come from the action and auteur ends of the industry. Hathaway's have tended to come from literary adaptations and studio comedies. Daytona is an odd place for those two résumés to meet, which is precisely why the photograph works.

## Levine, Staples, and the Slot After *Digger*

**Jonathan Levine** directs a **Will Staples** script, a hire that fast-tracked the movie as Cruise's next starring gig after Alejandro G. Iñárritu's ***Digger*** (**Oct. 2**). That sentence does a lot of calendar work. *Digger* lands in early October. *Verity*, Hathaway's other October title, lands the same day. The Thunder sequel is not a 2026 or 2027 release; it is a summer 2028 event, which means the intervening years belong to other movies and to the long, expensive process of shooting a racing picture for giant screens.

Levine is not the hire a studio makes if it wants a carbon copy of Tony Scott's smoke-and-neon style. He is the hire a studio makes if it wants a director who can keep a star vehicle from collapsing into pure machinery. Staples, for his part, is the writer attached to a script that Paramount is treating as locked enough to date and advertise. Plot details stay sealed, which in modern franchise language usually means the studio has a story it does not want reduced to a racing-movie logline before the first trailer.

The sequencing matters. Cruise has spent the last several years treating theatrical exhibition as a personal cause, arguing — on stages, in interviews, and by example — that movies should still be events people leave the house for. A *Days of Thunder* sequel shot for IMAX is consistent with that campaign. It is also consistent with the way his producing operation now works: find the next physical canvas, attach the people who can fill it, and put a date on the wall early enough that exhibitors and international distributors can plan around it.

## The Producers and the Premium-Format Bet

Cruise produces with **Jerry Bruckheimer** and **Tommy Harper**. Bruckheimer is the name that connects the sequel to the original more cleanly than any plot point could. He was the industrial force behind the 1990 film and behind the broader Cruise-and-speed brand that *Top Gun* created. Bringing him back is a signal to the audience that this is not a reboot with a new Cole Trickle and a new studio logo. It is a continuation, or at least a reunion, of the team that made the first one.

Harper's presence on the producing line is the other half of that signal. Cruise's recent theatrical work has depended on a producing bench that knows how to move a production through practical locations, IMAX cameras, and the insurance nightmares that come with putting a global star near fast vehicles. A NASCAR sequel will require all three.

### IMAX First, Then the Premium Stack

The technical plan is explicit. The film **will be shot for IMAX** and play **Dolby Cinema**, **4DX**, and other premium formats. That is not a throwaway line in a press release. Shooting *for* IMAX is different from finishing a standard digital capture and punching it up for a giant screen. It means the production is committing, at the camera-body level, to a frame that exhibitors can sell as an event. Dolby Cinema and 4DX extend that bet into sound and into the kind of seat-kicking, rumbling presentation that racing footage almost cannot help but justify.

Premium formats have become the economic argument for mid-budget-looking adult movies that still want tentpole play. A *Days of Thunder* sequel is not a superhero film and it is not a *Mission: Impossible* stunt reel. It is a star drama about cars. The only way that movie opens like a franchise title in 2028 is if the studio can tell audiences there is a version of it they cannot get at home. IMAX, Dolby, and 4DX are that version.

## Daytona as Setting and Statement

Cruise did not post a logo. He posted Daytona. The choice is both obvious and pointed. Daytona International Speedway is the cathedral of American stock-car racing, the place where the season's most famous 500 miles are run and where a movie camera can point at a banked turn and look like it is pointing at a national myth. Leaning two movie stars on a stock car in that place is a way of saying the sequel will not be shy about the sport.

It is also a way of managing expectations. A sealed plot can start to sound like a film with nothing to show. A photograph of Cruise and Hathaway on the car solves that problem without giving away a single scene. The audience now knows the texture: sun, sheet metal, the oval, two famous faces who have never shared a movie. Everything else — who wins, who crashes, what the team owner wants, whether Trickle is still the man he was in 1990 — can wait for the first footage.

NASCAR itself has spent the years since the original film becoming more camera-ready, more branded, and more willing to treat Hollywood as a partner rather than a tourist. A major studio sequel with Cruise in the cockpit and Hathaway in the owner's suite is the kind of crossover the sport has always wanted and the kind of theatrical event Paramount can still build in an era when many adult dramas go straight to streaming.

## What a 2028 Trickle Has to Be

Cole Trickle in 1990 was a young star's idea of a racer: gifted, reckless, in need of a crew chief and a reason to grow up. Cole Trickle in a film dated June 2, 2028, cannot be that person. Cruise is not that person. The interesting question, and the one the sealed plot is presumably built around, is what a driver looks like when the sport has professionalized, when the cars are rolling laboratories, and when the most powerful person in the frame may be the owner-engineer rather than the man wearing the firesuit.

That is why Hathaway's role description is doing so much work. A team owner who is also an engineer is not a cameo. It is a second protagonist. It suggests the sequel understands that modern racing is as much about data, setup, and organizational power as it is about nerve. Scott's film was about nerve. Levine's film, if the casting is any guide, will have to be about both.

The original's box office — **$82.6 million** in North America, **$157.9 million** around the world — is not a number a 2028 studio chases for its own sake. It is a reminder that this title already worked once as a commercial object, and that the people who remember it are now the age of the ticket-buyers who still go to IMAX on opening weekend. Nostalgia is a marketing tool. It is a poor substitute for a movie. Paramount's job between now and 2028 is to make sure the Daytona photograph is the start of a production, not the high point of a reunion.

## The Calendar Between Here and the Grid

Between Saturday's announcement and the June 2, 2028 date sits a stretch of years that will be filled with other Cruise and Hathaway movies. *Digger*, from **Alejandro G. Iñárritu**, arrives Oct. 2 and is the last Cruise starring vehicle before this one in the publicly described queue. Hathaway's *Verity* shares that October date, while *The Devil Wears Prada 2*, *The Odyssey*, and *The End of Oak Street* keep her on screens in the meantime. The Thunder sequel is the movie they will make after those obligations, not instead of them.

That gap is useful. Racing pictures eat time. They require access, safety plans, second-unit armies, and a star who is willing to sit in a car that is actually moving. Cruise's producing partnership with Bruckheimer and Harper exists for exactly this kind of schedule. Levine and Staples exist to make sure the schedule still produces a drama and not only a highlight reel.

For now, the public has what Cruise chose to give it: a locked studio deal, a date, a director, a writer, a producing trio, a premium-format plan, a sealed plot, and a photograph of two movie stars on a stock car in Daytona. It is the first time those two stars have been paired. It is nearly 38 years after Trickle first hit the track. And it is, if the Saturday lock holds, the most expensive argument yet that *Days of Thunder* was never only a memory of 1990. It was a character waiting for a second lap.
