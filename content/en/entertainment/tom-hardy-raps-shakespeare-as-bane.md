# Tom Hardy Raps Shakespeare As Bane

**Tom Hardy** has spent fifteen years proving he can disappear into a voice. On Friday he disappeared into a name. Under the alias **Frankie Pulitzer**, the British actor dropped a **15-track** rap album, a collaboration with the underground trio **Czarface** — **7L & Esoteric** plus Wu-Tang's **Inspectah Deck** — titled ***Czarface Meets Frankie Pulitzer***. Features include **Busta Rhymes**, **Method Man**, and **EL-P**. The verses are stuffed with movie and comic Easter eggs. The moment that will travel is the one that sounds like a joke until you sit with it: on **"Grim-Visaged War,"** Hardy recites Shakespeare's *Richard III* monologue in the muffled **Bane** voice from *The Dark Knight Rises*.

He has not spoken publicly as Frankie Pulitzer. He does not need to. The record is doing the talking, and it is talking in the private language of a movie-obsessed rapper who has been at this longer than the blockbuster career that made his face famous.

## An Alias With a Pedigree

Frankie Pulitzer is new as a headline. It is not new as a Hardy project. He has appeared on prior Czarface records, the kind of underground guest spots that never make the entertainment-weekly circuit and therefore never have to be explained. Before that, he recorded an unreleased **1999 mixtape** as **Tommy No. 1** that leaked in **2018**. The chronology matters. Hardy was rapping before *Inception*, before *Warrior*, before Bane, before the *Venom* movies turned him into a multiplex brand. The Pulitzer alias is a continuation, not a midlife hobby announced on a talk show.

### From Tommy No. 1 to Frankie Pulitzer

He told the **BBC** in **2011** he started rapping at **14** and *"wasn't very good."* That line has been sitting in the archive for a decade and a half, waiting for a Friday night when the same man would release fifteen tracks with Inspectah Deck and then, on one of them, become Bane long enough to do Shakespeare. The self-deprecation is useful. It keeps the album from being sold as a vanity project by a movie star who discovered a booth. It is closer to the opposite: a movie star returning to a booth he never really left.

Czarface is the right partner for that return. The trio — Boston producers and rappers 7L and Esoteric, locked to Inspectah Deck of the Wu-Tang Clan — has spent years making comic-book-steeped, sample-heavy records that treat pulp and hip-hop as the same art form. Their catalog is a museum of masked villains, Saturday-morning villains, and the kind of liner-note humor that rewards people who still own physical media. Putting Hardy inside that world is not a crossover stunt. It is a booking. He already spoke the dialect.

## The Guest List Is the Argument

Busta Rhymes, Method Man, and EL-P are not random luxury features. They are a map of the record's ambitions. Method Man is Wu-Tang blood and a living argument that a rapper can also be a screen actor without either job becoming a costume. Busta Rhymes is velocity and showmanship, the guest you call when a track needs a second weather system. EL-P is the producer-rapper whose career, from Company Flow through Run the Jewels, has been a long defense of dense writing and ugly, beautiful drums.

Together they place *Czarface Meets Frankie Pulitzer* on a specific shelf: not a film-adjacent mixtape, not a charity single, but an underground album that happens to have a movie star as one of its writers. That distinction will be lost on some of the people who click the Bane clip. It will not be lost on the people who already own Czarface records.

Fifteen tracks is a full album, not a curiosity EP. That length is a commitment. It means Hardy and the trio had enough material — enough verses, enough Easter eggs, enough voices — to justify the old-fashioned album shape in a year when most celebrity music arrives as a single and a making-of clip. The making-of clip, if it exists, has not been the story. The story is the record, and the record is crowded.

## Easter Eggs From the Video Store

The verses are stuffed with movie and comic Easter eggs, and the ones already in circulation read like a syllabus. **Willem Dafoe's Green Goblin**. Scorsese's ***Bringing Out the Dead***. **Doctor Doom**. And ***Dog Day Afternoon*** on **"Raw Forever."** That is a coherent taste, even if it looks like a pile. Dafoe's Goblin is the comic-book performance that taught a generation of blockbuster actors they were allowed to be feral. *Bringing Out the Dead* is late-nineties Scorsese at his most exhausted and fluorescent, a New York ambulance movie that has always belonged to night-shift obsessives. Doctor Doom is Marvel's aristocrat of grievance. *Dog Day Afternoon* is Al Pacino sweating through a bank robbery that turns into a media circus.

Hardy has lived in all of those neighborhoods as an actor — the masked villain, the nocturnal city, the comic-book tyrant, the man who cannot get out of a scene once it starts. Hearing him write them into rap verses is less a cameo than a confession of influence. "Raw Forever" using *Dog Day Afternoon* is the tell. That film is about performance under pressure, about a person who becomes more himself the longer the cameras stay on. A rapper who also happens to be one of the most transformed actors of his generation does not drop that title by accident.

On **"Mad Technology"** he name-checks ***Cujo*** and ***Misery***, then delivers an **X-rated Kathy Bates** line already circulating as the most clipped bar. The Stephen King pairing is doing double duty. *Cujo* is the animal that cannot be reasoned with. *Misery* is the fan who cannot be escaped. Kathy Bates's Annie Wilkes is one of the great screen monsters of the last forty years, and Hardy — who has played monsters, and who has been the object of a certain kind of fandom — turning her into an explicit punchline is the album at its most reckless. The clip will be the thing people send. The King references will be the thing people who stay for the whole track remember.

None of this requires Hardy to be a great rapper. It requires him to be a specific one: a writer who treats movies and comics as a second vocabulary, then puts that vocabulary in front of Inspectah Deck and trusts the beats to hold it.

## The Bane Monologue

The sticky moment is **"Grim-Visaged War."** The title is already Shakespeare; the phrase lives in *Richard III*, in the opening weather report of a play about a man who decides that if he cannot be a lover he will be a villain. Hardy recites the *Richard III* monologue in the muffled Bane voice from *The Dark Knight Rises*. That is the sentence that will outlive the album's chart position, if it has one.

Bane, as Hardy played him in Christopher Nolan's 2012 Batman film, was a study in obstructed speech. The mask was a medical device and a theatrical one. It turned every line into a rumble. Audiences complained, then quoted it, then turned the voice into a meme that has had a longer life than some of the film's plot points. To bring that voice back, not for a sequel and not for a Super Bowl ad, but for the opening of a Shakespeare history play, is the kind of choice only a performer who is genuinely amused by his own instrument would make.

### A Villain's Weather Report

*Richard III* begins with a man explaining himself to the audience. He is deformed, overlooked, and done pretending. Bane, in Nolan's film, is a man who explains himself through a mask and a revolution. Putting those two speeches in the same mouth is not subtle. It is also not empty. Both characters are theatrical. Both understand that power is a performance. Both address the crowd as if the crowd had asked for a confession.

"Grim-Visaged War" will be excerpted, remixed, and stripped of the verses around it. That is the cost of a sticky moment. The surrounding album is the argument that the moment is not a one-off gag. It is what happens when a rapper who used to be Tommy No. 1, who has already been on Czarface records, who started at fourteen and told the BBC he was not very good, decides that the most honest way to do Shakespeare is in the voice the world already associates with him.

## Silence as Strategy

Hardy has not spoken publicly as Frankie Pulitzer. In an industry that usually rolls out a celebrity album with a late-night story, a podcast confession, and a carefully lit studio photograph, the silence is a choice. It keeps the alias from collapsing into the actor. It also keeps anyone from asking him to rank his own bars against Method Man's, or to explain the Kathy Bates line, or to say whether Bane is back.

The silence has a history. Hardy has never been a fluent public explainer of his own process. He has preferred the work — the weight cuts, the accents, the roles that require a new walk — to the press cycle that follows it. Frankie Pulitzer fits that pattern. The name is a partition. On one side is the man who still has movies to promote. On the other is the 15-track collaboration with 7L, Esoteric, and Inspectah Deck.

The 1999 mixtape that leaked in 2018 already proved the partition is porous. People who wanted to hear Hardy rap did not wait for a sanctioned alias. They found Tommy No. 1. *Czarface Meets Frankie Pulitzer* is, in that sense, a legalization. The hobby is now a title. The title has guests who do not need Hardy's fame. The fame will sell the first click. The verses will have to sell the rest.

## Why the Record Makes Sense

Actor-rapper projects usually fail in one of two directions. They are too polished, sanded down by pop producers until the celebrity sounds like a guest on his own song. Or they are too timid, a spoken-word cameo that never risks a bar. This album, on the evidence of the Easter eggs and the Bane cut, is aiming at a third direction: overstuffed, referential, a little rude, and built with people who already know how to make a record that does not care about the movie business.

Czarface's entire project has been to treat comic-book mythology as folk music for people who grew up in comic shops. Hardy arriving with Green Goblin, Doctor Doom, *Misery*, *Cujo*, *Dog Day Afternoon*, and *Bringing Out the Dead* is not a tourist reading a cue card. It is a film actor writing from the stack of titles that built him. The X-rated Kathy Bates line on "Mad Technology" is the same instinct without the safety catch.

Shakespeare is the high-culture alibi, and it is also the most honest booking on the track list. Hardy trained as a stage actor. *Richard III* is a role that actors of his generation are supposed to want. He has now done a piece of it in the voice of a comic-book terrorist, on a rap album, under a fake name, without a press conference. That is a stranger and more interesting career move than another prestige limited series.

The 2011 BBC remark — that he started at 14 and *"wasn't very good"* — hangs over the whole enterprise as a kind of permission slip. He said the quiet part years ago. Friday's album is the loud part. Whether Frankie Pulitzer becomes a continuing alias or a one-album ghost will depend on whether Hardy wants another partition, and whether Czarface wants another fifteen tracks. For now the public has the record, the features, the clipped Bates bar, and a muffled king of England explaining, once again, why the winter of discontent is over.

Bane reciting Shakespeare should not work. On "Grim-Visaged War," it is the reason people will still be talking about Frankie Pulitzer after they have forgotten the rest of the Friday release pile. The rest of the album is there for anyone who wants to know what the actor sounds like when the mask is a name instead of a prop.
