# US deports Afghan to Central Africa

The **United States** on *Saturday* deported **dozens of people** to the **Central African Republic**, a landlocked country in the middle of the African continent whose capital, **Bangui**, is a long way from Kabul, Tehran, Kathmandu, or Managua. Among those on the aircraft, according to lawyer **Alma David** and the advocacy group **Human Rights First**, was an **Afghan man in his early twenties** whose **brothers collaborated with the U.S. military**.

That last fact is the hinge of the story. The young man was not, on the public record assembled by his lawyer and by Human Rights First, a stranger to the American war in Afghanistan. His family had already paid for that association. A **U.S. judge** had **blocked sending him back to Afghanistan** over **Taliban persecution fears**. **AP-reviewed court documents** said his family received **Taliban threats because of that U.S. work**. One brother now **lives in the United States after serving in the Afghan National Army**. Another, a **U.S.-trained pilot**, was **killed by the Taliban**.

He was sent anyway — not home, which a court had taken off the map, but to *Central Africa*, on a flight that **Savi Arvey** of Human Rights First said **landed in Bangui** carrying **12 Afghans**, **eight Iranians**, and **people from Nepal and Nicaragua**. Advocates say the **Trump administration** has used **often-secret third-country deals** to send **thousands** to **about two dozen nations that are not their own** — a *workaround* for people **protected from return home**. Saturday's flight was the **second to CAR since June**.

## A Removal That Was Not a Return

Deportation, in the ordinary American usage, means sending someone back. The word carries a geography: the country of citizenship, the place on the passport, the airport whose signs are in a language the deportee already knows. Saturday's operation did not do that. It moved **dozens of people** to the **Central African Republic**, a state that is not Afghanistan, not Iran, not Nepal, and not Nicaragua.

That distinction is the policy. **Alma David** and **Human Rights First** identified among the passengers an **Afghan man in his early twenties**. A federal judge had already **blocked sending him back to Afghanistan** because of **Taliban persecution fears**. If the United States could not put him on a plane to Kabul, the remaining options, as the administration has constructed them, were to keep him in the United States or to find another government willing to take him. Bangui was the second of those options.

**Savi Arvey** of Human Rights First said the flight that **landed in Bangui** held **12 Afghans** and **eight Iranians**, plus **people from Nepal and Nicaragua**. The manifest, as she described it, was a map of several crises at once: Afghans whose country is again under the **Taliban**; Iranians whose government is a long-running adversary of Washington; Nepalis and Nicaraguans whose presence on the same aircraft underlines that the destination was not chosen to match nationality. The destination was chosen because it would take them.

Advocates describe this as a *workaround*. People **protected from return home** cannot, as a legal matter, be dumped in the country they fled. They can, if another government agrees, be dumped somewhere else. The **Trump administration**, those advocates say, has used **often-secret third-country deals** to send **thousands** to **about two dozen nations that are not their own**. Saturday was one more run of that machine. It was also, specifically, the **second** such flight **to CAR since June**.

## The Family the Court Already Knew

The Afghan passenger is not a composite. He is a man **in his early twenties**, identified by **Alma David**, his lawyer, and by **Human Rights First**. What is known about him comes in large part from **AP-reviewed court documents** and from the fact of a **U.S. judge** who had already ruled on the danger of Afghanistan.

Those documents said his **family received Taliban threats because of** his brothers' **U.S. work**. **Collaboration with the U.S. military** is not a vague social fact in Taliban-run Afghanistan. It is a targeting criterion. One brother **served in the Afghan National Army** and now **lives in the United States**. Another was a **U.S.-trained pilot** and was **killed by the Taliban**. The surviving brother's U.S. residence and the dead brother's U.S. training are not ornamental details. They are the reason a judge treated a return to Afghanistan as a persecution problem rather than a routine removal.

The court did not, on the record described by David, Human Rights First, and the Associated Press, authorize a transfer to Bangui. It **blocked sending him back to Afghanistan**. That is a narrower order than a grant of asylum and a broader one than silence. It says: not *there*. It does not, by itself, say: not *anywhere*.

The gap between those two sentences is where third-country removal lives. If the legal protection is against *refoulement* — return to the place of feared harm — then a government that wants the person gone will look for a third map. The **Central African Republic** is that third map. It is not the country of the **Taliban**. It is also not the country of the **Afghan National Army** veteran who already lives in the United States, and it is not a place where a **U.S.-trained pilot**'s brother can be said, on the public facts, to have any tie.

## Bangui, Not Kabul

**Savi Arvey** of **Human Rights First** is the source for who was on the plane when it **landed in Bangui**. She counted **12 Afghans**. She counted **eight Iranians**. She said there were also **people from Nepal and Nicaragua**. Combined with the **dozens** reported as the size of the overall deportation, the picture is a mixed-nationality removal to a single African capital.

Bangui is the political and administrative center of the **Central African Republic**. It is not a traditional resettlement hub for Afghans. It is not, in the public facts of this case, a place the young man's family chose. The choice was made in Washington, under a **Trump administration** practice that advocates say relies on **often-secret third-country deals**.

Secrecy, in that description, is not a flourish. If the deals are *often-secret*, then the people being moved, their lawyers, and the courts that have already ruled on persecution may not see the receiving country's assurances in time to test them. **Alma David** was able to say that her client was among the deportees. A **U.S. judge** had already been able to say that Afghanistan was too dangerous. What neither the lawyer's public account nor the **AP-reviewed court documents** supplied was a judicial finding that the **Central African Republic** was safe, suitable, or even known to the passenger in any meaningful sense.

The flight's passenger list, as Arvey described it, also undercuts any claim that Bangui was selected as a cultural or linguistic match. **Twelve Afghans** and **eight Iranians** do not become Central African by landing. **People from Nepal and Nicaragua** do not either. The common feature is not origin. It is that the United States wanted them out and had a partner willing to let the plane land.

## A Workaround With a Pattern

Advocates put Saturday in a larger frame. They say the **Trump administration** has used **often-secret third-country deals** to send **thousands** of people to **about two dozen nations that are not their own**. The number *thousands* is not a rounding of this one aircraft. It is the claimed scale of the program. *About two dozen nations* is not a synonym for the Central African Republic. It is the claimed width of the network.

A workaround, in this usage, is a legal design. U.S. and international refugee norms treat return to persecution as a distinct harm. If a **U.S. judge** has **blocked sending** a person **back to Afghanistan over Taliban persecution fears**, the administration cannot, without defying that block, put him on a Kabul-bound flight. It can, if advocates' description of the policy is right, look for a third country that will accept him *as someone else's problem*. The **Central African Republic** has now accepted that role at least twice: Saturday's flight was the **second to CAR since June**.

The second-flight detail matters. A single landing can be described as an experiment, a one-off, a diplomatic improvisation. A second landing in a span that runs from **June** to *Saturday* is a route. Routes get used. **Dozens of people** on this aircraft, **12** of them **Afghans**, **eight** of them **Iranians**, others from **Nepal** and **Nicaragua**, are the human content of that route on this day.

Human Rights First is not a government agency. It is an advocacy organization speaking through **Savi Arvey** about the Bangui landing and, with **Alma David**, about the Afghan passenger. The **Associated Press** reviewed **court documents** rather than merely repeating a press release. Those documents, as described, are the independent paper: **Taliban threats** to the family **because of that U.S. work**; a brother in the **Afghan National Army** now in the **United States**; a brother who was a **U.S.-trained pilot** and is dead at the **Taliban**'s hands; a judge who treated those facts as reason to keep Afghanistan off the itinerary.

## What Protection Meant, and What It Did Not

The young man is **in his early twenties**. That age, stated by his lawyer and by Human Rights First, places him in the generation that came of age as the American war in Afghanistan ended and the **Taliban** returned to power. He is old enough to be deported as an adult. He is young enough that the brothers who **collaborated with the U.S. military** are the generation that did the collaborating.

Protection from return home, as advocates use the phrase, is the floor. It is the thing a court can order when the evidence of **Taliban persecution fears** is strong enough, and when **AP-reviewed court documents** show **Taliban threats** tied to **U.S. work**. It is not, in the events of Saturday, a right to remain in the United States. It is not a family-reunification ticket to join the brother who **lives in the United States after serving in the Afghan National Army**. It is not compensation for the brother who was a **U.S.-trained pilot** and was **killed by the Taliban**.

It is a prohibition with a hole in it. The hole is named Bangui.

**Alma David** and **Human Rights First** put the Afghan man on the public record so that the hole would have a face: early twenties, brothers in the American war, a judge's block, a third-country landing. **Savi Arvey** put the rest of the plane on the record so that the hole would have a passenger count: **12 Afghans**, **eight Iranians**, **people from Nepal and Nicaragua**, among **dozens** sent to the **Central African Republic**. Advocates then put the hole in a system: **often-secret third-country deals**, **thousands** of people, **about two dozen nations that are not their own**, a **workaround** for those **protected from return home**, and a **second** flight **to CAR since June**.

None of those elements requires a new quotation. The people on the record are **Alma David**, **Savi Arvey**, **Human Rights First**, an unnamed **U.S. judge**, the **Associated Press** as reviewer of **court documents**, the **Trump administration** as the author of the deals, and the unnamed Afghan man and his brothers. The numbers on the record are **dozens**, **early twenties**, **12**, **eight**, **thousands**, **about two dozen**, and **second**. The places on the record are the **United States**, **Afghanistan**, the **Central African Republic**, **Bangui**, **Nepal**, **Nicaragua**, and the implied geography of **Iran**. The armed groups and institutions on the record are the **Taliban**, the **U.S. military**, and the **Afghan National Army**.

## The Second Flight Since June

Saturday was not the first time. The same corridor — United States to the **Central African Republic** — had already been used **since June**. Advocates' accounting makes this the **second** such flight. Whatever diplomatic arrangement opened Bangui as a receiving point was still open. Whatever legal theory treats a third country as a substitute for home had not been withdrawn.

That continuity is the news inside the news. A one-time transfer of **dozens of people**, including **12 Afghans** and **eight Iranians** and **people from Nepal and Nicaragua**, would still raise the questions **Alma David** and **Human Rights First** are raising about a man **in his early twenties** whom a judge would not send to the **Taliban**. A *second* transfer says the questions did not close the airport.

The **Trump administration** has not, in the facts assembled for this account, published the text of the CAR arrangement. Advocates call such deals **often-secret**. Secrecy is useful to a government that is improvising receiving states. It is costly to a lawyer trying to tell a court where a client will be by nightfall. David's client was, by Saturday, no longer in a posture where a block on Afghanistan could be confused with a block on removal. He was in Bangui, or on his way from the tarmac into the custody of a state that is not his own.

The brother who **lives in the United States after serving in the Afghan National Army** remains, on these facts, in the United States. The brother who was a **U.S.-trained pilot** remains dead, **killed by the Taliban**. The family that **received Taliban threats because of that U.S. work** remains a family that a **U.S. judge** found too endangered for Kabul. The young man remains the person who was **protected from return home** and was sent to *Central Africa* instead.

## What Saturday Leaves on the Record

The United States deported **dozens of people** to the **Central African Republic** on Saturday. **Alma David** and **Human Rights First** said one of them was an **Afghan man in his early twenties** whose **brothers collaborated with the U.S. military**. A **U.S. judge** had **blocked sending him back to Afghanistan over Taliban persecution fears**. **AP-reviewed court documents** said the **family received Taliban threats because of that U.S. work**. One brother **lives in the United States after serving in the Afghan National Army**. Another, a **U.S.-trained pilot**, was **killed by the Taliban**.

**Savi Arvey** of Human Rights First said the flight **landed in Bangui** with **12 Afghans**, **eight Iranians**, and **people from Nepal and Nicaragua**. Advocates say the **Trump administration** has used **often-secret third-country deals** to send **thousands** to **about two dozen nations that are not their own**, a *workaround* for people **protected from return home**. Saturday's flight was the **second to CAR since June**.

Those sentences are the whole of the verified file. Everything else is the meaning of the file: that a judicial shield against Afghanistan did not shield against Africa; that collaboration with the **U.S. military** was enough to get a family threatened and a pilot killed, and not enough to keep the surviving youngest of that story in the country that trained the pilot; that **Bangui** is now, for the second time since **June**, a destination of convenience for people the United States will not send home and will not keep.

The **Central African Republic** did not become Afghanistan by receiving Afghans. **Iran**, **Nepal**, and **Nicaragua** did not become nearer to Bangui because their nationals shared a cabin. The **Taliban** did not become less interested in families tied to the **Afghan National Army** and to **U.S.** training because the youngest brother was flown in the opposite direction. What changed on Saturday was location. What did not change is the reason a judge had already said *not Afghanistan* — **persecution fears**, **Taliban threats**, **U.S. work**, a living soldier-brother, a dead pilot-brother — and the reason advocates say thousands of others are being moved the same way: because a third country will say yes when home is legally closed and the administration still wants the plane to leave.
