# Deadly Trump-Shaped Pills Spark Dutch Alert

## A novelty stamp and a delayed poison

The Dutch have spent decades treating the night-time drug market as something that can be watched rather than wished away. That culture of surveillance, built around walk-in testing rooms and a national early-warning network, is why a handful of odd-looking tablets can still move a government institute to its highest public alarm. This month they did.

The **Trimbos Institute**, the Netherlands' centre for mental health and addiction, issued a national **Red Alert** after testers found two-tone ecstasy tablets molded like President Donald Trump's head, with *"NL"* and *"Trump"* stamped across a break line on the back. Several pills were turned in at Dutch testing centers in August. The **Netherlands Forensic Institute** analyzed one as well. The chemistry, not the caricature, is what triggered the alarm: labs found a very high dose of **PMMA**, a slower-acting *MDMA* analog that often lacks the expected buzz, so users may take another pill and overdose.

Trimbos warned of delayed nausea, a racing heart, convulsions, and fatal overheating, and said not to use the pills under any circumstance. The warning is not a metaphor. PMMA has a well-documented habit of harming people who think they have taken ordinary ecstasy and, when nothing happens, take more.

## What testers actually found

The tablets are designed to be recognized. Two tones. A presidential profile. A scored reverse that reads like a souvenir and a serial number at once: NL on one side of the break line, Trump on the other. In a market that has long used logos, cartoon characters, and political faces as branding, the stamp is a sales pitch. It is also, for harm-reduction workers, a tracing device. When several of the same objects show up at testing centers in a single month, the network can treat them as a batch rather than as isolated curiosities.

That is what happened in August. Users, or people close to them, brought the Trump-head tablets to Dutch testing services rather than swallowing them untested. The samples were consistent enough for Trimbos to treat the find as a national event, not a local rumor. The Netherlands Forensic Institute's parallel analysis added a second laboratory signature to the same object. Both streams of work pointed to the same conclusion: these were not high-dose MDMA pills with a novelty press. They were PMMA, and the dose was very high.

A Red Alert is the institute's way of telling the entire country, at once, that a particular product on the illegal market is too dangerous to gamble with. It is reserved for substances whose pharmacology, dose, or combination of the two puts users at immediate risk of serious harm. The Trump tablets met that bar. The publicity is the intervention. People who will never visit a testing room may still see a photograph of a two-tone presidential head and decide not to swallow one.

## Why PMMA is not MDMA

*Para-methoxymethamphetamine*, the compound abbreviated as PMMA, sits in the same chemical neighborhood as amphetamine and MDMA, which is why it can be pressed into a tablet, sold as ecstasy, and swallowed by someone who has no reason to suspect a substitution. It is not MDMA. The distinction is not academic.

MDMA, the drug most users think they are buying when they buy a pressed tablet in a Dutch club or at a festival, typically announces itself within an hour: warmth, a lifted mood, a sense that music and other people have more texture than they did on the walk in. The timeline is part of the folklore. People plan around it. They wait. If the wait feels long, many of them, especially in a loud room with friends who already seem high, take another half or another whole.

PMMA disrupts that script. It is a slower-acting analog. The expected buzz is often weak or late or both. The body, meanwhile, is already dealing with a stimulant that can drive heart rate up, scramble temperature regulation, and, at high doses, push a person into seizures. Trimbos's list of warning signs is the clinical version of that mismatch: delayed nausea, a racing heart, convulsions, and fatal overheating. Hyperthermia is the outcome harm-reduction agencies fear most. A person overheating on a dance floor can look, for a while, like someone who is merely too hot, too sweaty, too "in it." By the time the difference is obvious, cooling them down is a medical emergency.

The compound has circulated as an ecstasy substitute on European markets on and off for years, sometimes alongside its close relative *para-methoxyamphetamine*, or PMA. Both have a reputation among chemists and street-level testers for a cruel mismatch between marketing and effect. They can be made in the same kinds of illicit labs that produce amphetamine-type stimulants. They can be dosed inconsistently from tablet to tablet. They can leave a user convinced that nothing has happened until, quite suddenly, too much has happened. None of that requires a presidential mold. The mold only makes the product easier to describe, easier to seek out, and easier to share.

### The second-pill problem

The specific danger Trimbos is describing is not only that PMMA is toxic. It is that PMMA is toxic in a way that invites a second dose. A user who has paid for ecstasy, who has arranged a night around it, and who feels little or nothing after the usual interval is making a decision under social pressure and incomplete information. The rational move, from inside that night, is to assume the first pill was weak. The rational move, from outside it, is to wait a long time or not to have taken an untested tablet at all.

Very high dose makes the trap tighter. Even a single tablet can be enough to cause serious harm. A second tablet, taken because the first seemed to do nothing, can turn a survivable exposure into an overdose. That is why the institute's instruction is absolute: do not use the pills under any circumstance. There is no safer way to take a Trump-head tablet from this batch. There is no recommended half. Splitting the score that reads NL on one side and Trump on the other is not harm reduction. The advice is not to moderate. It is to stop.

European drug-checking services have been repeating a version of this lesson whenever PMMA reappears. The pattern is grimly stable: a press that looks like a party, a compound that does not feel like one, and a cluster of overheating cases that can arrive at hospitals hours after people thought they had simply bought a dud. The chemistry is old. The presidential mold is new. The risk is the same.

## A testing culture inside an illegal market

Ecstasy is illegal in the Netherlands. Possession, production, and sale are criminal offenses. That legal fact sits beside another one that still surprises visitors: the state funds, and publicly defends, a system in which people can have illegal pills analyzed and be told what is in them. The point is not to bless the market. It is to keep the market from injuring people in the dark.

The 2024 government report that put annual ecstasy use at about **550,000** people is the demographic backdrop for that choice. In a mid-sized European country, that figure describes a large, ordinary cohort — festival-goers, students, club regulars — not a tiny subculture that can be lectured out of existence. Dutch policy has, for a generation, treated widespread MDMA use as a public-health fact. Testing centers, coordinated in a national monitoring system that Trimbos helps to run, are how that fact is turned into data: which logos are circulating, which doses are climbing, which adulterants have arrived from which production runs.

When several Trump-head tablets landed on those counters in August, the system did what it was built to do. It matched the physical description. It ran the chemistry. It compared notes with the forensic institute. It issued a Red Alert instead of a quiet internal memo. The Dutch model is often caricatured abroad as permission. It is better understood as instrumentation. The state cannot see into every pocket. It can, when a testing center and a forensic lab agree on a very high dose of PMMA in a tablet shaped like a president, tell the public the truth in time for the truth to matter.

There is a second, quieter function. Testing data feeds European early-warning networks. A novelty press that appears in Amsterdam or Rotterdam this month can appear in a German or Belgian club next month. A national alert is also a cross-border signal. The tablets do not care about the border. The warning, if it is loud enough, can travel with them.

## No confirmed victims, and why that does not close the file

The most important sentence in the current alert is also the most easily misread. Spokesperson **Anniek Groothuis** told the Associated Press there were no known deaths or serious illnesses tied to these batches and no victims with PMMA in their blood. That is a statement about confirmed harm, not about the absence of risk.

It means, as of her comments, that Dutch authorities had not identified a patient whose toxicology matched these tablets and whose condition matched the PMMA profile. It does not mean the pills are safe. It does not mean nobody has taken them. It does not mean a delayed presentation — a collapse at home, hours after a night out — could not still appear. Overheating and seizures do not always arrive on the dance floor. They can arrive in a taxi, in a bedroom, in the slow hour when a second pill finally stacks on top of the first.

Groothuis's assurance is still news. Alerts of this kind often follow harm rather than precede it. The Dutch testing pipeline, in this case, appears to have caught the batch while the medical ledger was still empty. That is the best version of the story, and it is also the reason the institute is talking so loudly. The window in which an alert can still be preventive rather than retrospective is short. Tablets move. They are shared. They cross borders in jacket pockets. A presidential novelty press is, among other things, a memorable object people like to show their friends.

The absence of known victims with PMMA in their blood also says something about how this particular alarm was built. It was built from pills handed in, not from hospital charts. Several samples at testing centers, plus a forensic-institute analysis, were enough. That is a system working at the front of the problem rather than the back. It will only keep working if people continue to treat an unusual tablet as something to identify rather than as something to swallow on reputation.

## Political faces as product design

This is not the first time a Trump face has been used to sell a stimulant. German police seized thousands of orange Trump-head tablets in 2017, marketed as *"Trump makes partying great again."* That slogan was a joke with a distribution network behind it: a recognizable head, a campaign catchphrase, a color, a rumor that the pills were strong. Seizures of that size are a reminder that political iconography is useful to illegal manufacturers for the same reason it is useful to anyone else who sells a branded product. People remember it. They ask for it by name. They photograph it.

The 2017 German tablets and the 2026 Dutch finds should not be collapsed into a single conspiracy. Presses travel. Designs get copied. A mold that worked as a novelty nine years ago can be recut, restyled in two tones, and sent through a different supply line with a different payload. What the two episodes share is not a proven common source. It is a marketing logic. The face on the pill is meant to be the story. The story, this time, is the chemistry underneath.

For testers, the design is a mixed blessing. It makes the product easier to describe in a public warning. It also makes the product more likely to be sought out by people who think a presidential stamp is a guarantee of quality, or at least of a good anecdote. Trimbos's message cuts against that instinct. The stamp is not a mark of craft. It is a way to recognize something that should be thrown away.

Illegal stimulant markets have always borrowed from popular culture because popular culture is a ready-made language of desire. Footballers, cartoon animals, luxury logos, and heads of state have all, at various times, been pressed into pills that had no relationship to the image on the face. Buyers are not purchasing a political statement. They are purchasing an expected duration of MDMA. When the image is this famous, the expected duration can become a rumor that outruns the lab. The Red Alert is an attempt to put the lab back in front of the rumor.

## What the alert is asking people to do

The instruction is simple on purpose. Do not use the pills. Do not assume that a friend who always tests has tested *these*. Do not split a two-tone Trump head down the NL/Trump score and call that caution. If someone has already taken one and feels delayed nausea, a racing heart, or any slide toward overheating or convulsions, the next step is medical, not pharmacological. There is no at-home correction for PMMA. There is cooling, monitoring, and a hospital.

The deeper request is aimed at a country that already knows how to do this. Bring unknown tablets in. Treat a novelty press as a reason for suspicion, not a reason for confidence. Understand that a missing buzz is not a refund and not a prompt to double the dose. In a market that serves about 550,000 people a year, the difference between a Red Alert that is heeded and one that is treated as background noise is measured in ambulances.

None of this resolves the larger Dutch argument about ecstasy, a drug that remains illegal and widely used, tested and still dangerous, culturally ordinary and periodically lethal. The Red Alert does not pretend to. It answers a narrower question: what do you do when the object in circulation is a very high dose of a slower-acting analog, shaped like a president, scored with a nationalist abbreviation and a surname, and capable of convincing a person that the first pill failed? You tell them, as Trimbos has, not to use it under any circumstance. The caricature is on the pill. The emergency is in the delay.
