Long-Term Melatonin Use Linked to 90% Higher Heart Failure Risk in Major New Study

Melatonin supplements have become one of the most widely used over-the-counter aids for sleep problems. Marketed as a “natural” hormone that helps regulate the body’s internal clock, they are available in pharmacies, grocery stores, and online retailers with minimal regulation in many countries. A large observational study published in late August 2026 has now raised a significant safety concern: people who used melatonin long-term for chronic insomnia showed approximately a 90 percent higher risk of developing heart failure over a five-year follow-up period.

The findings have prompted immediate discussion among cardiologists, sleep physicians, and public-health researchers. While the study does not establish a direct causal link, the magnitude of the association is large enough to warrant careful attention and further investigation.

Study Design and Key Results

Researchers examined electronic health records and prescription or supplement-use data from a substantial cohort of adults diagnosed with chronic insomnia. Participants were divided into groups based on reported or documented melatonin use lasting months to years, versus those who did not use the supplement. After adjusting for age, sex, body-mass index, existing cardiovascular risk factors, and other sleep medications, the long-term melatonin users exhibited a markedly elevated incidence of new-onset heart failure.

The relative risk increase of roughly 90 percent translates into a clinically meaningful absolute difference given the size of the population that relies on melatonin. Secondary analyses suggested the association was stronger among individuals who used higher doses or continued supplementation for several consecutive years.

Importantly, the study authors emphasized that the results are preliminary and observational. Residual confounding—differences between users and non-users that were not fully captured in the data—cannot be ruled out. People who turn to long-term melatonin may already have more severe or treatment-resistant insomnia, which itself is linked to cardiovascular stress.

Possible Biological Mechanisms

Several plausible pathways could explain an association between prolonged melatonin exposure and heart failure risk:

  • Effects on blood pressure and vascular tone — Melatonin influences nitric-oxide pathways and can alter nocturnal blood-pressure dipping patterns. Disruptions in these rhythms have been tied to cardiac remodeling.
  • Hormonal and metabolic interactions — Chronic exogenous melatonin may interfere with the body’s own production or with related endocrine axes that affect fluid balance and cardiac workload.
  • Impact on sleep architecture — While melatonin can help some people fall asleep, its effects on deeper sleep stages and overnight autonomic balance remain incompletely understood. Poor-quality sleep is itself a risk factor for heart failure.
  • Formulation and dosage variability — Over-the-counter melatonin products vary widely in actual content and purity. Some contain higher doses than labeled or additional compounds that could contribute to cardiovascular effects.

None of these mechanisms has been definitively proven in the context of the new findings, but each is consistent with existing physiological knowledge and merits targeted laboratory and clinical study.

Context: Melatonin’s Rise and Regulatory Status

Melatonin occupancy of the sleep-aid market has grown dramatically over the past decade. Many consumers view it as safer than prescription hypnotics because it is a hormone the body already produces. In the United States and several other countries it is sold as a dietary supplement rather than a drug, meaning manufacturers face lighter safety and efficacy requirements.

Sleep experts have long cautioned that melatonin is best suited for short-term use—jet lag, shift-work adjustment, or occasional sleeplessness—rather than indefinite nightly consumption. The new data reinforce that caution and suggest that chronic users, particularly those with existing cardiovascular risk factors, should discuss continued use with a physician.

Clinical Perspectives and Patient Guidance

Cardiologists interviewed about the study stressed that patients should not panic or abruptly stop medication without medical advice. Heart failure is a multifactorial condition, and melatonin is unlikely to be the sole driver in any individual case. Nevertheless, the signal is strong enough that clinicians may begin asking specifically about long-term melatonin use during cardiovascular risk assessments.

Practical recommendations emerging from expert commentary include:

  • Prefer non-pharmacological approaches—cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), sleep-hygiene optimization, and treatment of underlying conditions—as first-line strategies.
  • If melatonin is used, favor the lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary duration.
  • Individuals with hypertension, prior heart disease, or multiple cardiovascular risk factors should consult a doctor before starting or continuing long-term supplementation.
  • Report any new symptoms such as unexplained fatigue, shortness of breath, or swelling to a healthcare provider promptly.

What Happens Next

The research community is already calling for prospective randomized trials that can better isolate melatonin’s effects from those of insomnia itself. Such trials will need to track both hard cardiovascular outcomes and detailed measures of sleep quality, autonomic function, and circulating biomarkers.

Regulatory agencies may also review labeling and consumer guidance. Some countries already treat higher-dose melatonin as a prescription product; the new evidence could accelerate similar moves elsewhere or prompt clearer warnings on supplement packaging.

A Call for Balanced Caution

Melatonin remains a useful tool for many people when used appropriately. The August 2026 findings do not invalidate its short-term benefits. They do, however, highlight that “natural” does not automatically equal risk-free, especially when a hormone is taken at pharmacologic doses for years.

Patients, clinicians, and researchers now share a clearer mandate: treat chronic insomnia seriously, prioritize proven behavioral therapies, and approach long-term hormonal supplementation with the same scrutiny applied to other medications that affect the cardiovascular system. The heart, it appears, may be more sensitive to nightly melatonin than previously assumed.
