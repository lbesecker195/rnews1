Keto Diet Slashes Liver Fat by 67% in Landmark Clinical Trial—Outperforms Mediterranean and Low-Fat Approaches

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) has quietly become one of the most common chronic conditions worldwide, closely tied to the global rise in obesity and type 2 diabetes. A clinical trial reported in late August 2026 has delivered striking results: participants following a ketogenic diet experienced an average 67 percent reduction in liver fat, substantially greater than the reductions seen with either a Mediterranean diet or a traditional low-fat diet.

Importantly, all three dietary groups lost roughly the same amount of body weight—about 10 percent of baseline weight—yet the keto group showed superior improvements in several key metabolic markers. The findings are already prompting re-evaluation of dietary guidelines for patients with fatty liver disease.

Trial Design and Participant Profile

The study enrolled adults diagnosed with obesity, prediabetes, and imaging-confirmed fatty liver. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three isocaloric dietary patterns for a defined intervention period:

  • A well-formulated ketogenic diet low in carbohydrates and moderate in protein and fat, designed to induce nutritional ketosis.
  • A Mediterranean diet rich in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, olive oil, fish, and nuts.
  • A conventional low-fat diet emphasizing reduced total fat intake and increased carbohydrate from whole-food sources.

All groups received comparable behavioral support, portion guidance, and monitoring. Liver fat was quantified using advanced imaging techniques at baseline and study end. Secondary outcomes included insulin sensitivity, lipid profiles, inflammatory markers, and patient-reported measures of adherence and quality of life.

Standout Results for the Ketogenic Arm

While weight loss was similar across arms, the ketogenic group achieved the largest reduction in hepatic steatosis—approximately 67 percent on average. Many participants moved from the fatty-liver range into the normal range. Improvements in fasting insulin, HOMA-IR (a measure of insulin resistance), and certain liver enzymes were also more pronounced on keto.

Researchers noted that the benefits appeared relatively early and were sustained through the trial period. Adherence rates were acceptable across groups, though some participants in the keto arm reported an initial adjustment phase commonly described as “keto adaptation.”

Why Might Keto Work Better for Liver Fat?

Several mechanisms are under discussion:

  • Reduced insulin levels — Lower carbohydrate intake decreases insulin secretion, which in turn reduces the drive for hepatic fat synthesis (de novo lipogenesis).
  • Increased fat oxidation — Nutritional ketosis is associated with greater reliance on fatty acids for energy, including those stored in the liver.
  • Changes in gut microbiome and bile-acid signaling — Emerging data suggest ketogenic diets alter microbial metabolites that influence liver metabolism.
  • Calorie quality versus quantity — Even with matched weight loss, the macronutrient composition appears to matter for liver-specific outcomes.

These pathways are consistent with earlier smaller studies and animal models, but the current trial provides higher-quality human evidence.

Safety and Practical Considerations

The trial reported no serious adverse events attributable to the ketogenic diet. Common transient effects—such as fatigue, headache, or constipation during the first weeks—were managed with electrolyte guidance and dietary adjustments. Longer-term safety, particularly regarding lipid profiles in susceptible individuals and micronutrient adequacy, continues to be monitored in follow-up work.

Clinicians emphasize that a ketogenic diet is not appropriate for everyone. People with certain metabolic conditions, those taking specific medications (including some diabetes drugs), pregnant or breastfeeding individuals, and patients with a history of disordered eating require individualized assessment. Medical supervision is strongly recommended when using keto therapeutically for fatty liver or diabetes.

Implications for Dietary Guidelines and Clinical Practice

For years, low-fat and Mediterranean patterns have been the default recommendations for metabolic liver disease. The new data do not invalidate those approaches—both produced meaningful weight loss and some liver-fat reduction—but they demonstrate that a properly constructed ketogenic diet can deliver superior hepatic results in the short-to-medium term.

Hepatologists and endocrinologists are beginning to discuss how best to incorporate these findings into practice. Possible shifts include:

  • Offering keto as a structured, time-limited intervention for patients with significant steatosis who are motivated and medically suitable.
  • Combining dietary strategies sequentially (for example, keto for rapid liver-fat reduction followed by Mediterranean for long-term maintenance).
  • Greater emphasis on continuous glucose monitoring and ketone tracking to personalize carbohydrate restriction.

Looking Forward

Larger and longer trials are already being planned to confirm durability of the liver-fat reductions, assess cardiovascular outcomes, and compare different degrees of carbohydrate restriction. Researchers are also exploring whether intermittent or cyclical ketogenic approaches can capture the benefits while improving long-term adherence.

For the millions of people living with NAFLD—many of whom progress silently toward inflammation, fibrosis, and cirrhosis—the August 2026 trial offers a hopeful message: dietary composition can exert powerful effects on liver fat independent of overall weight loss. The ketogenic diet, once viewed primarily as a tool for epilepsy or rapid weight reduction, is gaining recognition as a potentially potent metabolic therapy when applied thoughtfully and under professional guidance.

Patients interested in exploring this approach should consult a knowledgeable physician or registered dietitian rather than attempting extreme restriction on their own. With proper support, the dramatic reductions in liver fat observed in the trial may become achievable for a much wider population.

Individual Variation and Personalization

Not every participant on the ketogenic diet achieved the same degree of liver-fat reduction. Exploratory analyses suggested that baseline insulin resistance, age, sex, and genetic factors related to lipid metabolism influenced individual responses. These observations reinforce the growing recognition that dietary prescriptions should be personalized rather than one-size-fits-all.

Some patients may thrive on a well-formulated ketogenic approach for a defined period and then transition to a more moderate carbohydrate Mediterranean-style pattern for long-term sustainability. Others may find that a less restrictive low-carbohydrate diet delivers adequate benefits with better adherence. The clinical trial provides a strong evidence base for offering keto as an option; it does not mandate that every patient with fatty liver must follow it indefinitely.

Practical Guidance for Clinicians and Patients

For physicians and dietitians considering the ketogenic diet in the context of NAFLD, several practical points emerge from the trial and related literature:

  • Emphasize whole-food sources of fat and adequate protein rather than processed “keto” products.
  • Monitor electrolytes, kidney function, and lipid panels, especially during the adaptation phase.
  • Ensure sufficient fiber and micronutrient intake through non-starchy vegetables and, when appropriate, targeted supplementation.
  • Set clear time frames and reassess liver fat, metabolic markers, and patient preference at regular intervals.
  • Integrate behavioral support to improve adherence and address the social and practical challenges of dietary change.

Patients should approach any significant dietary shift as a medical intervention rather than a casual lifestyle experiment, particularly when liver disease or diabetes is present.

The Bigger Picture in Metabolic Health

The August 2026 trial adds to a broader reassessment of carbohydrate restriction in metabolic disease. While energy balance remains fundamental, the quality and composition of the diet clearly influence organ-specific outcomes such as hepatic steatosis. The ketogenic diet’s ability to produce large reductions in liver fat even when weight loss is matched to other diets underscores the importance of metabolic context, not merely calories.

As research continues, the field is moving toward more nuanced, phenotype-driven nutrition recommendations. For the substantial population living with fatty liver disease, that evolution offers the prospect of more effective, better-tolerated dietary tools—and the hope that progression to advanced liver disease can be slowed or prevented through carefully chosen nutritional strategies.
