Belichick Silences Critics With Dublin Upset

Sport · August 29, 2026 · 9 min read

Bill Belichick opened Year 2 at North Carolina with a 15-10 win over TCU in the rain at Aviva Stadium in Dublin, revenge after last year’s 48-14 Labor Day blowout that started a 4-8 debut. The score is small. The setting is not. A wet Saturday in Ireland is a strange place to quiet an American argument, and it was enough.

New quarterback Billy Edwards Jr. threw for 232 yards. TCU led 10-3 after two drives and never scored again, going scoreless on its last eight possessions. The Tar Heels took a 12-10 halftime lead on a safety when a punt snap sailed over John Hoyet Chance’s head — UNC’s first Power Four halftime lead of the Belichick era. Two late fourth-down stops inside the 10 sealed it. Critics get a two-week pause before East Tennessee State.

Rain in Dublin, and a 48-14 memory

Aviva Stadium is a rugby ground that American football borrows when the sport wants a spectacle and a time zone. Dublin in late August can do rain without asking. Year 2 of the Belichick experiment opened in that weather, against the same TCU program that had used last year’s Labor Day to announce that the coach’s NFL résumé would not automatically transfer to Chapel Hill.

The 48-14 blowout was not a footnote. It was the first data point of a 4-8 debut, the game that told a restless fan base what the rest of the autumn would feel like: overmatched, behind, and short of the standard the name on the headset was supposed to guarantee. Labor Day is supposed to be a beginning. Last year it was a verdict that arrived before the first Monday of September.

Revenge, in college football, is usually a homecoming or a conference rematch. This revenge was transatlantic. Same opponent. Different continent. A year of roster and scheme work in between. The 15-10 final is not a 48-14 in reverse. It is something colder: a game TCU had in its hands after two drives and then could not finish, in the rain, against a defense Belichick called himself.

A 4-8 debut is the kind of record that produces the critics the headline is talking about. Year 1 did not look like a dynasty in waiting. It looked like a hard job in a Power Four league that does not grade on the curve of six Super Bowl rings. Year 2 had to start somewhere. It started in Dublin, at 15-10, with the rain doing what rain does to passing games and to the mood of a crowd that had flown a long way to see whether the name still worked.

Edwards, two TCU drives, and then silence

Edwards Jr. is the new quarterback. He threw for 232 yards. That is not a video-game line and not a disaster. In a 15-10 game, in the rain, 232 yards is the number of a quarterback who did enough and did not have to do everything. The Tar Heels did not outscore TCU 48-14. They outlasted a team that scored on its first two drives and then stopped.

TCU led 10-3 after two drives. Openers are supposed to look like that for a program that had scored 30-plus in 10 straight openers. The Horned Frogs came into Dublin with a decade-minus habit of treating the first Saturday as a scoring exhibition. Ten-three after two possessions is how those exhibitions begin. The next eight possessions are how this one ended: scoreless.

Going scoreless on the last eight possessions is a defensive fact, not a special-teams accident. It means the second half, and the back of the first, belonged to the side that could not put more points on the board. It means TCU’s opener streak of 30-plus points died in the rain, short of the number, short of the feel of those prior 10 openers. A team that scores 10 and stops has not been unlucky. It has been answered.

Edwards’s 232 yards sit next to that shutdown rather than in competition with it. North Carolina did not need him to throw the game over the stadium. It needed him to keep the chains honest while a defense, called by a 74-year-old head coach filling in for his son, took the air out of TCU’s remaining script. In a 15-10 final, the quarterback’s job is to avoid becoming the story. Two hundred thirty-two yards, in the rain, against a team that had just scored 10 in two drives, is how you avoid becoming the story.

A snap over the punter’s head

The Tar Heels took a 12-10 halftime lead on a safety when a punt snap sailed over John Hoyet Chance’s head. Two points. A wet ball. A snap that did not stay in the operation. UNC’s first Power Four halftime lead of the Belichick era arrived not on a touchdown celebration but on a mistake in TCU’s punting game.

A safety is the most neglected score in football until it happens. It is two points and the ball. It is also, in a 10-3 game that had already started to stall, a way to go in front without needing a long drive to finish. The snap sailed. Chance, the punter, had the ball go where punters hope it never goes: over the head, toward the end line, into the kind of scramble that ends with two points on the other side of the scoreboard.

First Power Four halftime lead of the Belichick era is a brutal little phrase. It means Year 1 did not produce one. It means every previous time North Carolina had reached the break against a Power Four opponent under this staff, the Tar Heels had been tied or behind. Dublin, in the rain, against the team that had humiliated them on Labor Day, was the first time the locker room at half belonged to them on the scoreboard.

12-10 is not a cushion. It is a lead you can lose on one clean TCU drive. The fact of it still mattered. Belichick’s North Carolina had not had this feeling against this tier of opponent at the break. They had it because a snap went where it should not have gone, and because the prior TCU scoring had already stopped.

Two stops inside the 10

Two late fourth-down stops inside the 10 sealed it: Jeremy Payne stuffed on fourth-and-1 from the 5, then Jaden Craig incompleted on fourth-and-2 from the 10. That is the game, restated as two plays. TCU, scoreless since those first two drives, got the ball close enough to take the lead and could not take it.

Fourth-and-1 from the 5 is a play a rushing team expects to convert. Payne was stuffed. A yard, from the 5, with the opener on the line in a 15-10 game, and the body that needed to fall forward did not. Goal-line defense is the oldest Belichick product. It does not require a new vocabulary. It requires the front to win a short-yardage snap when the offense has already declared it will not kick.

Then, later or in the same desperate sequence the facts describe as the other of two late stops: fourth-and-2 from the 10, Jaden Craig incompleted. Incomplete is the cleaner failure. No yards. No play under review for a fingernail. A throw that did not find a receiver, from the 10, on fourth down, with TCU still hunting the points it had stopped scoring after the second drive of the day.

Inside the 10, twice, on fourth down, late. That is how a 15-10 win over a team that had scored 30-plus in 10 straight openers is finished. Not with a walk-off score. With two possessions that died in the shadow of the goal line. The rain does not show up in the play-by-play of a stuff and an incompletion. The defense does.

Defense without Steve, a broadcast without Hudson

Belichick, 74, called defense while Steve Belichick is on medical leave. The head coach is not a ceremonial presence on this staff in the ordinary season, and he was not one in Dublin. Calling defense is the work he has always been famous for. Doing it at 74, in Year 2 of a college rebuild, while his son and defensive lieutenant is on medical leave, is the version of that work the critics had not yet seen succeed in a Power Four opener.

Medical leave is not a schematic detail. It is an absence. The coordinator’s chair was not filled by a new hire for the purposes of this game’s public accounting. It was filled by the head coach. The two fourth-down stops inside the 10, the eight scoreless TCU possessions after the first two drives, the first Power Four halftime lead — those are the products of a defense called by Belichick because Steve Belichick was not there to call it.

The ESPN broadcast barely mentioned Jordon Hudson. That sentence will mean more to people who have followed the sideshow than to people who only watch the snaps. Hudson has been a running subplot of the Belichick-at-Carolina era, the kind of personal-life noise that television producers sometimes cannot resist. On Saturday, in Dublin, in the rain, with a 15-10 upset unfolding, the broadcast barely mentioned her. The game was allowed to be a game. The coach, afterward, did not fill the vacuum with a speech about himself.

“I’m just happy for the players,” Belichick said. It is a cliché until it is the only quote, and it is the only quote on the record. He did not use the win to settle with the 4-8 people. He did not use it to narrate the medical leave. He did not use it to talk about Hudson. He pointed the sentence at the roster that had just stuffed Payne on fourth-and-1 from the 5 and forced Craig into an incompletion on fourth-and-2 from the 10. Happy for the players is what a 74-year-old coach says when the night is finally a win and he would like the night to stay about football.

Thirty-plus no more, and two weeks to wait

TCU had scored 30-plus in 10 straight openers. That streak is over. Ten, not 30-plus. Eight possessions in a row with nothing. A safety given away on a snap over Chance’s head. Two fourth-down failures inside the 10. The opener that was supposed to look like the previous 10 looked, instead, like a defensive tape Belichick can take into the next meeting.

Critics get a two-week pause before East Tennessee State. That is a specific calendar, not a general amnesty. North Carolina does not play again next Saturday and then the Saturday after in a gauntlet. There is a two-week pause. Then East Tennessee State. The critics who spent a year on the 4-8 debut and the 48-14 Labor Day tape will have fourteen days in which the last result is 15-10 in Dublin, in the rain, with Edwards at 232 yards and a defense that did not give TCU another score after 10-3.

A pause is not a conversion. East Tennessee State is not TCU, and TCU in Week 1 is not the rest of a Power Four season. The facts do not include a conference title or a rewritten legacy. They include a revenge win, 15-10, at Aviva Stadium. They include a first Power Four halftime lead of the Belichick era, obtained on a safety. They include a 74-year-old head coach calling defense on medical-leave duty. They include an ESPN broadcast that barely said Hudson’s name. They include the quote he did give.

Year 2 had to start with something other than 48-14. It started with something other than 48-14. The rain in Dublin will dry. The two-week pause will end. East Tennessee State will arrive on the schedule, and the critics will come off pause whether the next score looks like this one or like last Labor Day. For one wet night in Ireland, the players he said he was happy for had a 15-10, and the 10-straight 30-point openers had a zero in the remaining column. That is as much silence as a 74-year-old coach gets. He used it on the defense, and on the players, and on not much else.
