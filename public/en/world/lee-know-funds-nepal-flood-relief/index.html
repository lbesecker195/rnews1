Lee Know Funds Nepal Flood Relief

World · August 29, 2026 · 14 min read

JYP Entertainment said Aug. 30 that Stray Kids member Lee Know donated 100 million won (~$75,000) to World Vision for emergency relief after catastrophic Nepal-Tibet flash floods. The announcement is brief, as agency statements about an idol’s private giving usually are. The facts inside it are not. The gift, delivered Aug. 28, is earmarked for food, nutrition, temporary housing, and safe water and sanitation for children and families hit by the disaster. It arrives while Nepal and Tibet still search for thousands missing after glacier-triggered floods killed hundreds along the border.

Lee Know did not hold a press conference. He spoke through his company. “I hope my small contribution can offer some comfort to those going through difficult times,” he said via JYP, wishing victims and relief workers “a peaceful daily life.” Those two sentences are the entire public voice of the donation. Everything else is a ledger: the amount, the date the money moved, the NGO that received it, the uses it is locked to, and a record of giving that now stretches more than a decade.

A gift delivered before it was announced

The sequence matters. The money left on Aug. 28. The public learned on Aug. 30. Two days is not a long embargo, but it is a choice. Humanitarian agencies prefer to have funds in hand, or at least committed, before a celebrity’s name is attached to a disaster, because the attachment itself becomes a story that can outrun the logistics. JYP’s Aug. 30 statement treats the donation as a completed act, not a pledge still traveling through a bank. World Vision is the recipient. The purpose is emergency relief. The geography is the Nepal-Tibet disaster zone created by catastrophic flash floods.

One hundred million won is, in the conversion JYP’s disclosure supports, about $75,000. That is not a figure that rebuilds a valley. It is a figure that, if it is spent the way it is earmarked, can buy a defined set of things in the first weeks after water has taken houses, kitchens, wells, and roads. The earmark is unusually specific for a celebrity gift of this size. It is not a general “use as needed” envelope. It is food, nutrition, temporary housing, and safe water and sanitation, and it is aimed at children and families hit by the disaster.

That list is the grammar of a flood response. Food is the first shortage people feel when markets are cut off and stored grain is wet or gone. Nutrition is the narrower, clinical cousin of food — the supplements, the feeding programs, the attention to children whose diets collapse faster than adults’. Temporary housing is the phrase agencies use when a house is no longer a house and a tarpaulin, a tent, or a rented room has to stand in. Safe water and sanitation are the public-health half of the same emergency: wells contaminated, latrines swept away, diarrhea waiting behind thirst. World Vision’s mandate has always leaned toward children. The earmark follows that mandate rather than fighting it.

Lee Know called the sum a small contribution. Relative to a Himalayan disaster that has already killed hundreds and left thousands unaccounted for, the description is accurate. Relative to what a single entertainer can move in two days without a telethon or a matching campaign, it is the size of gift his own history with the same NGO has made familiar.

Glacier, water, and a border that floods both ways

The disaster the money is meant to meet is not a seasonal monsoon story in the ordinary sense. JYP’s statement, and the description that travels with it, locate the catastrophe in glacier-triggered floods along the Nepal-Tibet border. Glacier ice, high lakes, and steep valleys are a known combination in the high Himalaya. When a lake held by ice or moraine fails, or when a surge of melt and debris drops into a river corridor, the water does not stay on one side of a line drawn on a map. It becomes a flash flood: sudden, heavy, and indifferent to the difference between a Nepali village and a Tibetan one.

The human accounting, as far as the public record attached to this donation goes, is still unfinished. The floods killed hundreds along the border. Nepal and Tibet still search for thousands missing. Those two numbers — hundreds dead, thousands missing — are the entire casualty frame available here. They are already large enough. A missing person after a flash flood is not a paperwork delay. It is a body that water, mud, and distance have not yet returned, and a family that cannot bury or stop looking. Search is the work that continues after the cameras have a death toll they can repeat. The K-pop star’s latest gift lands inside that unfinished search, not after it.

Catastrophic is the right adjective for a flood that does this much this fast. Flash floods in mountain country take bridges, cut roads, and isolate hamlets that were never easy to reach. Relief then becomes a problem of access as much as of money. Food cannot be eaten if it cannot be carried in. Temporary housing cannot be raised if the ground is still a river. Safe water is a technical job when every stream is brown. An earmark that names those four needs is an earmark written for the first phase of a mountain flood, when the question is whether children eat, whether families have a roof that is not the sky, and whether the water they drink will not make the disaster medical as well as geological.

The border itself is part of the story. Nepal and Tibet meet along a high, cold edge of the plateau. Communities on both sides live with the same ice and the same rivers. A glacier-triggered flood does not respect which government issues the identity cards. Relief, by contrast, is almost always organized by jurisdiction: one set of agencies on the Nepali side, another on the Tibetan side, with international NGOs threading through where they are allowed. World Vision’s work in this gift is described as emergency relief for children and families hit by the disaster. The statement does not itemize which side of the line each dollar will touch. It names the disaster as Nepal-Tibet, which is already a way of saying the water did not choose.

World Vision, 2014, and the youngest Honor Club member

Lee Know has backed World Vision since 2014. That is a long relationship for a pop star whose public life, for much of that span, has been the life of a trainee and then a member of Stray Kids, the JYP group that made his stage name a global one. Twelve years of backing an NGO is not a one-off photo. It is a habit that predates the fame that now makes a 100 million won transfer into international news.

In 2024 he gave another 100 million won to World Vision’s Global Food Crisis Response, becoming the NGO’s youngest Bob Pierce Honor Club member. The two gifts — 2024’s food-crisis donation and this week’s flood relief — are the same size. They are not the same file. One was aimed at a worldwide hunger emergency. One is aimed at a specific mountain flood. Both went to the same organization. Both sit at the 100 million won mark that, inside World Vision’s Korean donor culture, is the threshold associated with the Bob Pierce Honor Club.

Bob Pierce founded World Vision. The Honor Club that bears his name is the agency’s way of recognizing donors who give at a level the organization treats as transformational rather than casual. Lee Know’s 2024 gift made him the youngest person in that club. The current statement does not claim the Nepal-Tibet donation changes that status; it does not need to. The point of repeating the 2024 gift in the same breath as the 2026 one is continuity. This is not a celebrity discovering charity because a disaster is trending. This is a donor who has been inside the same NGO since 2014, who crossed a major recognition line in 2024 with a 100 million won gift to the Global Food Crisis Response, and who has now sent the same amount again, two days before his company told the public, toward children and families in a Himalayan flood.

The Global Food Crisis Response was a hunger program. The Nepal-Tibet gift is a flood program. The through-line is not the hazard. It is food, children, and World Vision as the vehicle. Nutrition appears in this week’s earmark for the same reason food appeared in last year’s global response: children are the first to show the cost of a broken food system, whether the system broke because of a worldwide price shock or because a glacier sent a river through a kitchen.

Last year’s other ledger: a hospital and a cat rescue

The World Vision relationship is not the whole of the public giving. Last year he donated 200 million won split between Samsung Seoul Hospital patient care and a Goyang cat-rescue group. That sentence, attached to the flood gift in the same disclosure, is doing a different kind of work. It says the 100 million won to World Vision is not an isolated headline. It sits beside a larger, split gift to a major hospital’s patients and to animals in the city of Goyang.

Two hundred million won is twice this week’s flood donation. Split, it is two acts rather than one: care for patients at Samsung Seoul Hospital, and support for a cat-rescue group in Goyang. Neither is World Vision. Neither is a disaster on a foreign border. One is domestic medical care. One is domestic animal welfare. Together they sketch a donor who does not give only through a single international NGO and who does not give only when a camera is pointed at a flood. The hospital gift is about people who are already inside a Korean medical system. The Goyang gift is about cats. Fans of Lee Know have long associated him with cats; the statement does not need to belabor that. It simply records that a cat-rescue group in Goyang received part of a 200 million won split last year.

The pattern, laid out without embroidery, is three recent public numbers. In 2024, 100 million won to World Vision’s Global Food Crisis Response, and with it the Bob Pierce Honor Club as its youngest member. Last year, 200 million won split between Samsung Seoul Hospital patient care and a Goyang cat-rescue group. This week, another 100 million won to World Vision, delivered Aug. 28, announced Aug. 30, earmarked for food, nutrition, temporary housing, and safe water and sanitation after catastrophic Nepal-Tibet flash floods. A career of backing World Vision since 2014 sits under all three.

None of those figures should be inflated. None of them should be turned into a personality profile the statement does not authorize. They are enough, on their own, to show that the K-pop star’s latest gift is latest in a sequence, not first in a conversion narrative.

What the earmark is for, and what it is not

Earmarks are promises about use. They are also limits. Food, nutrition, temporary housing, and safe water and sanitation are the four doors this money is allowed to walk through. Reconstruction of permanent homes is not on the list. Livelihood replacement is not on the list. Search-and-rescue is not on the list, even though Nepal and Tibet still search for thousands missing. The gift is not a substitute for governments, armies, or the heavier machinery of disaster response. It is a contribution aimed at the household-level emergencies that follow a flood: hunger, the particular hunger of children, a place to sleep that is not a ruined room, and water that will not sicken the people who have already lost too much.

World Vision is built to work in that household register. The organization has spent decades running child-focused programs in which food and water and a safe place to stay are not metaphors. Channeling a celebrity donation through an agency that already has procurement, field staff, and accounting for those exact items is how a 100 million won transfer becomes rice, feeding, shelter materials, water tablets, latrines — the unglamorous inventory of relief — rather than a general fund that dissolves into overhead arguments. JYP’s statement does not publish a line-item budget. It publishes the earmark. That is the accountability the public is being offered.

One hundred million won, about $75,000, will not close a disaster that has killed hundreds. It will not find the thousands still missing. Lee Know’s own language does not pretend otherwise. He called it a small contribution and said he hoped it could offer some comfort to people going through difficult times. Comfort is a modest verb. It is the right verb for a gift this size in a catastrophe this large. The wish that victims and relief workers might have a peaceful daily life is equally modest. Daily life is what a flash flood destroys. Peaceful is what the missing cannot yet be said to have, and what the workers who are feeding, housing, and trucking water are trying to restore in fragments.

Relief workers are named in that wish on purpose. They are the people who will turn the earmark into crates and tanks and tarps. They are also, in a disaster that is still a search, among those living without anything that looks like a peaceful day. Including them in the hope is a small piece of accuracy. The victims are not the only people the flood is using up.

An idol, an agency, and a statement with no extra names

JYP Entertainment is the messenger because JYP Entertainment is the employer. Korean entertainment companies typically announce their artists’ donations in a short notice that names the amount, the recipient, and a quotation supplied by the artist. That is the form this disclosure takes. There is no named spokesperson in the public account beyond the company and Lee Know. There is no invented scene of him visiting a shelter. There is no claim that he traveled to Nepal or Tibet. The record is a transfer on Aug. 28, a statement on Aug. 30, a quotation, a wish, and a biography of prior gifts.

Stray Kids is the group; Lee Know is the member. The rest of the act is not part of this donation. The story is not a group charity single or a fan-driven matching drive, at least not as JYP has described it. It is one member sending 100 million won to World Vision for a specific emergency. Fans will do what fans do with information like this. The statement itself does not instruct them. It records the gift and the hope attached to it.

The quotation is worth taking at its word because it is the only quotation there is. “I hope my small contribution can offer some comfort to those going through difficult times.” Hope, small, comfort, difficult times: four hedges around a real transfer. He is not declaring that 100 million won will repair a border. He is saying he hopes the people living through the flood will feel some comfort from it. The second clause, the wish for victims and relief workers to have a peaceful daily life, is the same idea in a different tense — not the comfort of a package arriving, but the ordinary day that packages are supposed to make possible again.

There is a temptation, in writing about K-pop philanthropy, to treat every donation as branding. The public facts here cut against a pure branding read without requiring a saintly one. A relationship with World Vision since 2014 is older than the current news cycle. The Bob Pierce Honor Club membership came from a 2024 gift to a global food-crisis response, not from a flood that is in the headlines this week. Last year’s 200 million won went in part to cats in Goyang and in part to patients at Samsung Seoul Hospital, neither of which is a Nepal-Tibet photo opportunity. The simplest reading is also the one the numbers support: he gives, he has given in this range before, and this time the receiving file is a glacier-triggered flood that has already killed hundreds and left thousands missing.

The money and the missing

The K-pop star’s latest gift lands as Nepal and Tibet still search. That present tense is the hardest fact in the disclosure. Search means the disaster is not over. It means the death count of hundreds is a floor, not a finished census. It means children and families — the population the earmark names — include households that do not yet know who is coming home.

Food, nutrition, temporary housing, and safe water and sanitation are what you fund when people are alive and displaced and thirsty. They are not what you fund when you are still looking. Both jobs are happening at once. Governments and local crews search the mud and the river corridors. Agencies such as World Vision, if they are doing what this earmark describes, try to keep the living from becoming a second emergency of hunger and disease. Lee Know’s 100 million won is aimed at the second job. It does not pretend to do the first.

Glacier-triggered floods will happen again in this mountain system. That is a geophysical statement, not a prediction of the next date. Ice, lakes, and steep valleys are not a one-time arrangement. What a donor can do about that, from Seoul, on Aug. 28, is limited and specific. He can send 100 million won, about $75,000, to an NGO he has backed since 2014, lock it to food and nutrition and shelter and water, and ask, through his company two days later, that the people living inside the wreckage — and the people working in it — might be offered some comfort and, in time, a peaceful daily life.

That is the whole of the story JYP Entertainment told on Aug. 30. It is enough. The floods were catastrophic. Hundreds are dead along the Nepal-Tibet border. Thousands are missing. A Stray Kids member moved a familiar sum to World Vision before the public was told, named the uses, and declined to say more than a hope. The rest is work on the ground, which no press release can finish.
